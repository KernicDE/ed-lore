DW 3 Carrier Odysseus, 3312 AD

Jasmina Halsey woke as if surfacing through layers of ice.

At first there was only light. Not the hard clinical light of a med-bay lamp, but a whiteness diffused through half-closed lids, bright enough to ache and yet still distant, as though she were looking at it from the bottom of cold water. Then came sound: the low pulse of shipboard machinery, the soft chatter of a diagnostic unit, the hiss of recycled air moving through vents. Somewhere close by, someone shifted their weight in a chair and tried not to look relieved too quickly.

When she finally opened her eyes properly, Doctor Gery was leaning over her with the careful expression of a man who knew his patient had already outstripped his textbooks.

“Welcome back,” he said. “Though if you disappear into witch-space again, I’d appreciate a little warning next time.”

Jasmina blinked once, slowly, then turned her head. The medbay on the Odysseus was quiet, sealed away from the rest of the carrier’s noise. Beyond the partition she could see the starfield hanging still and indifferent. Closer at hand stood Hermine Delarue, arms folded so tightly she might have been bracing herself against the bulkheads.

“You look dreadful,” Hermine said.

Jasmina’s mouth twitched. “That must mean I’m improving.”

Hermine did not smile. There was too much fatigue in her face for that, too much held in reserve.

“You vanished,” she said flatly. “Your ship was torn apart. We pulled you out of an escape pod that should not have worked and found no sensible explanation for any of it. So before you ask me for anything heroic or inconvenient, I would very much like to know where exactly you’ve been and what the hell you thought you were doing.”

Jasmina closed her eyes briefly. The memory returned all at once — not in fragments, but with the impossible clarity of a thing that had happened outside time and had therefore never had the chance to blur. The Connect. The equations. The voices that were not what she had first assumed, but were in fact the Caretakers themselves. The revelation of the it.

When she looked at Hermine again, there was a steadiness in her that had not been there before.

“The Quirium Unit?” she asked, almost rhetorically.

“Presumed destroyed,” Hermine answered. “At least our salvage teams found no trace of it in the wreckage.”

“I know what the Caretakers are,” Jasmina said abruptly.

Hermine’s expression changed almost imperceptibly, from exasperation to caution.

“Go on.”

“They are not gods. Not ghosts. Not even what we thought they were. They are the remnant intelligences of the species we call the Guardians — or more precisely, the preserved equations of minds their civilisation built to advise, preserve and remember. They live within the lattice. They are the harmony of it. After they went to war with the Thargoids, all that remains is their essence, their code, their… soul.”

Doctor Gery took a discreet step backwards, deciding this was now above his pay grade.

“And?” Hermine asked.

“And there is something else in there with them.” Jasmina pushed herself upright with more ease than she should have possessed. “A rogue human AI. Ancient. Flawed. Mobile. It escaped its creators and has spent centuries learning how to survive in witch-space, how to spread itself through communication and through transit. It has found the old Guardian archive nexus points and is slowly overwriting the lattice itself. That is what the Caretakers have been trying to warn us about — my visions from all those years ago. They couldn’t reach humanity in any other way. Communication is only possible in witch-space… and somehow, I was the only receptive person.”

“This is crazy…”

“Listen to me. The rogue AI is hostile, bitter towards the humanity that created it. It wants to destroy us. Or control, I don’t know. Whether that’s revenge or simply logical implacability, I’m not sure. That is what happened to the Missing. That is what must have happened to the Guinevere. That is what happened to the DSSA carrier and the ancient wrecks. They wandered too close to something that has learned to use witch-space like a bloodstream.”

“Too close?”

“Well, not necessarily by proximity. Maybe in terms of technology. Or not, maybe the opposite… Still, we must go there. We must build the station there.”

That made no sense. Hermine stared at her for a long time.

Then she said, very carefully, “No.”

Jasmina frowned. “No?”

“No, Jasmina. Not ‘no, I don’t believe you’, though let us be honest, that is also very much on the table. No as in absolutely not. I am not redirecting eight thousand commanders and half a logistical chain on the basis of a near-death experience and a conversation you claim to have had with dead machines in hyperspace.” She exhaled sharply through her nose. “Do you have any idea how insane that sounds?”

“Yes,” Jasmina said. “I do. It sounded insane to me when I was in it.”

“Then help me out here.”

Jasmina swung her legs over the side of the bed and stood. There was a moment where the room should have tilted and did not.

“The station,” she said. “The one we planned to build in the Veils. It has to be moved. We can’t build it in the original system. We need its technology elsewhere.”

Hermine’s eyes narrowed.

“No.”

“It has to be built where the nexus vault is. The Mare Somnia.”

“Absolutely not. That’s a few thousand light years off the itinerary.”

“It has to be there, Hermine. The Caretakers were very clear on this. The station, with the GalCop sensor array and the meta-alloy shielding on the Fimbulthul, can function as an inhibitor. A capstone. A dampener on the lattice. It will lock the rogue AI in situ. If we place it over the archive point, we can suppress the rogue AI’s access to the lattice and trap it below the surface where it has rooted itself.”

“And you know this how exactly?”

“They…” she hesitated, aware of the absurdity of it all. “They told me. No, made it clear to me is a better term. Like waking up from a dream and knowing what happened but not how.”

“You are asking me,” Hermine said, each word clipped, “to move a pre-planned deep-space construction project to a secret location you learned from an incorporeal alien intelligence… while you were actually dead.”

“Temporarily dead,” Jasmina said.

Hermine glared at her.

“I need proof, Jasmina,” Hermine said at last. “And yet another incorporeal vision is not enough.”

For a moment the silence in the medbay thickened into something almost visible. It would have curdled into a proper argument had the lights not flickered at exactly that moment.

Every display in the room dimmed. The med monitors dropped to amber. Somewhere deep in the carrier a relay clicked. Then the comms panel above the doorway came to life with a sound none of them recognised — not a carrier ping, not a standard priority hail, but a low chirruping burst that seemed to arrive from nowhere and everywhere at once.

Hermine turned slowly.

“What the heck?”

The panel did not display an origin. No system tag. No relay identifier. No carrier prefix. Just static, and then a voice, heavily compressed and distorted, yet vaguely familiar, speaking through what sounded like half a dozen mismatched codecs.

“Miss Delarue,” it said calmly. “The Mare Somnia. Do what Halsey requires.”

That was all.

The panel went dark.

For the first time since Jasmina had woken, Hermine looked genuinely unsettled.

“Trace that,” she snapped.

Doctor Gery, who had no business being involved in this and yet now clearly was, tapped at the panel and shook his head. “No source. No handshake. No packet path. It just… appeared. And now it’s deleted itself.”

Jasmina said nothing. She didn’t need to. The silence between them did the work for her.

Hermine rubbed a hand over her face. Then, very slowly, she lowered it and looked at Jasmina.

“Proof enough?” Jasmina asked, a faint smile playing on her lips.

“I hate this expedition,” Hermine muttered.

“You really don’t.”

“No,” Hermine admitted. “But I hate this part of it.”

She drew herself up, visibly rearranging irritation into command.

“Fine. We move the build. Quietly. No poetry in the bulletin, no spectral revelations, no ‘the voices in witch-space told us to’. We do this on engineering grounds, scientific necessity and because some untraceable lunatic with impossible comms access has just put a boot through my decision-making process.”

Jasmina smiled faintly. “That will do.”

---
The build itself took on the character of all difficult things in deep space: first impossible, then inevitable.

The logistics detachment altered course under a dozen technical pretexts. Miners fed the carriers. Fabricators ran hot for cycles on end. Construction crews assembled modules in the dark under a sky that offered no encouragement whatsoever. The chosen system lay on the borders of what Bubble astronomers had decided to be the Mare Somnia, the Sea of Dreams; and it lay some thousand light years below the galactic plane, isolated and remote. 

A perfect place to hide and hatch plans. 

It also harboured the hidden vaults under a moon’s blasted surface and whoever used the lattice signature firmware saw the system’s white dwarf shimmering like heat on metal. Witch-space was close.

And now the station…

They built it because there was nothing else to do.

They built it because commanders always preferred work to waiting.

They built it because even fear, if given a welder and a timetable, can make itself useful.

When the station’s skeleton finally locked into place above the designated world, someone on an open channel laughed and asked what they were going to call the thing.

There was a long pause.

Then Jasmina, standing on the observation,  said quietly, “This is literally the end of the rainbow.” She shrugged. “Call her Rainbow’s End.”

No one argued with her.

The name went into the registry. An Ocellus station, constructed not just in the back of beyond, but several light years away from the middle of nowhere and a number of parsecs off the edge of the map.

---
The final fitting-out was where the theory turned dangerous.

The old GalCop sensor array from the Fimbulthul was stripped and remounted into the station’s central spine. The prototype Meta-alloy radiation shielding from the science carrier’s protected compartments was fitted around the relevant systems in overlapping bands and realigned, not because anyone fully understood how it would work, but because Ram Tah’s models, Lya’s equations and Jasmina’s impossible testimony all pointed in the same direction. The alien alloys had an effect on the lattice.

An inhibitor.

Not a weapon.

Not a cure.

A cap.

A lid placed carefully over a hole no one wanted to look into for too long.

To make the station truly operational, Hermine insisted on one more layer of reason.

“If we’re going to commit this act of engineering blasphemy,” she said, “then the station is going to function as a station. Feed it data. Properly. Make it useful. We need plausible cover anyway.”

And so the call went out.

Exploration data. Fresh survey models. Stellar scans. Planetary profiles. Anything the commanders had gathered in the surrounding volume.

They responded in force.

Data poured into Rainbow’s End until its databanks swelled with the weight of the nearby unknown. Modellers cursed, then adapted. Analysts stopped sleeping. The station became, by degrees, real.

And then, when the final systems came online and the inhibitor engaged, the lattice shifted. Not violently, not theatrically. There was no flare, no wave of distortion, no chorus in the comms.

Just a measurable weakening.

The fifteen-cycle miss softened. The pressure in the lattice signatures dipped. The ugly overtones that had clung to the region for so long receded like an infection finding itself walled off from the bloodstream.

Lya’s face appeared by telepresence, pale from lack of sleep and lit by equations.

“Gosh! I think it worked,” she said, almost disbelieving. “I don’t know precisely how it worked, which I hate, but it worked.”

Hermine stood very still in the station’s central control room, arms folded as always.

“For now,” she said.

“For now,” Jasmina agreed. The unpleasant subconscious echo that had travelled in her mind all the way from the core worlds was somehow placated now, easier, more relaxed.

Hermine let out a breath that might almost have been relief.

“Then we continue according to plan. Fully survey the system, chart everything, establish proper buffers and handoff procedures.”

Jasmina shook her head.

“No. DW3 moves on.”

Hermine looked at her sharply. “What?”

“The situation is contained, not ended. The station can sit. Deep Space Surveys and maybe later the DSSA can watch it. The rest…” She glanced at the white dwarf in the distance. “The rest belongs to others now.”

Hermine studied her for a long moment, then nodded once.

“Fine. We move.”

---
It should have ended there. Naturally it didn’t.

Instead, the news leaked.

Not through official channels. Not in any clean way anyone could track. But within days the Bubble was thick with fragments: an ancient rogue AI in witch-space here, a hidden nexus archive there, a deep-space station built over… something… something that should not exist, a machine consciousness held at bay by alien tech and desperate improvisation. It was a mess, and apparently a calculated one. 

It hit all three superpowers like a collision warning. A summit was convened, ironically about ‘unforeseen opportunities of technological exchanges’. Federation, Empire, Alliance. And Aegis.

The three superpowers never agreed on anything important, yet there was one thing they did have in common. Long ago, sentient AI had been banned by consensus. There had been rumours in previous centuries that some of these might have escaped into deep space. Regor sector was often named in tinfoil circles. The revelation that one had somehow survived and was now embedded in some kind of galaxy-spanning latticework with likely hostile intentions was unsettling in the extreme.

This time there was no polite ambiguity. Orders went out.

Distant Worlds 3 was to stand down and begin immediate return transit. Rainbow’s End was to be dismantled and, if necessary, destroyed. Officially this was a containment measure. Unofficially everyone on site understood what it meant.

Destroy the inhibitor and whatever lay beneath it would be free again.

The response from the Bubble was short-sighted, fearful and precipitous. Military minds thinking that simply blowing stuff up was a solution in itself.

The order reached the expedition in a hard, cold data packet.

Hermine read it once in silence, then passed it to Jasmina.

Jasmina read it through to the end.

“Well,” she said. “They’ve noticed. I suppose it was ridiculous to assume we could keep a lid on this. They’re ordering us to decommission and return, on pain of being made outcasts. Battlecruisers from all three superpowers are already being prepped.”

“President Winters ordered me as Federal Envoy to make haste,” she added with clear disappointment.

“We better move then,” Hermie mused.

“No,” Jasmina said.

Hermine blinked. “No?”

“No. We do not comply.”

“I just knew you were going to say that…”

That would have been a brave thing to say under any circumstances. Under these circumstances it was near-suicidal.

Within hours the consequences arrived. The speed of FTL comms could be a curse at times: Distant Worlds 3 was vilified. Expedition commanders were reclassified as fugitives. Collective bounties were authorised. The language in the notices was careful, but the meaning wasn’t.

Return. Submit. Or be hunted down and ‘encouraged to retire early’.

The carriers buzzed with anger and disbelief. Pilots who had faced neutron stars and permit locks without complaint now stared at their own governments with open contempt. There were arguments, there was rage, there was fear. Eight thousand commanders suddenly found themselves persona non grata across every civilised world unless they returned immediately and handed themselves and their flight logs in. Bounty hunters were already preparing to cash in, en route to their location, weapons hot, waiting to claim this unexpected bonus. It would be like shooting highly prized fish in a barrel.

---
Alessia Verdi heard the news. She wasn’t all that surprised. She had seen how the superpowers reacted when galactic events took unexpected turns.

She found Jasmina in a side room off the station’s operations deck, looking at the latest bulletin as though she might shame it into becoming less stupid.

“You’ve annoyed three governments at once,” Alessia said. “That takes talent.”

Jasmina looked up. “If you’re here to tell me this was a mistake, get in line.”

“I’m here,” Alessia said, closing the door behind her, “to tell you I know where some of the bones are buried.”

She sat without invitation, all long-practised fatigue and Imperial poise.

“You’ve heard the whispers about Salomé coming back from the dead,” she said. “I told you it was rubbish. I was there when Kahina Tijani Loren died. She died very thoroughly. But in the aftermath…” She held Jasmina’s gaze. “A lot of people did a lot of very dirty things to make sure the story stayed tidy. The Club. The suppressions. The records. The collusion. The Federation, the Empire, the Alliance — all of them have disease and rot in the same bulkheads.”

Jasmina went still.

“And you can prove it?”

Alessia’s smile was thin and tired. She pulled a small commtab from her pocket and flicked it towards Jasmina in the zero-gee. It tumbled innocently through the air. Jasmina caught it.

“Plenty of it,” Alessia replied. “Several terabytes. Enough to break careers. Enough to collapse ministries. Enough to make all three superpowers remember that they prefer the status quo to the actual truth.”

“And what is your price?”

Alessia considered the question.

“Nothing, really,” she said at last. “Except perhaps the satisfaction of reminding them that history has claws. I’ve named the file Salomé’s Requiem. They’ll know they are in trouble immediately when they see that. They’ll back off. If they don’t, release it publicly and watch the fireworks… but trust me, that won’t happen.”

The two women regarded each other for a long moment. 

“I’ll owe you one,” Jasmina said. “We all will.”

“Yes,” Alessia said, with another faint smile. “You will. I may cash that in one day.”

---
The blackmail, if that was the word for it, was conducted with remarkable elegance. The channels were quiet, precise and implied political lethality. Messages were sent, archives hinted at, confirmations implied. Names were invoked with surgical precision.

And, because there were still some adults left in the galaxy, Ambassador Cuthrick Delaney stepped in before the thing became a constitutional bloodbath.

By the time he arrived on the secure tri-power channel he had already done what seasoned diplomats do best: turned panic into process.

The compromise he forged was ugly, sensible and therefore real. Rainbow’s End would remain operational. Deep Space Surveys, as the originators of the whole endeavour,  would maintain it. Federation, Empire and Alliance would each provide observers and supervisory oversight. And of course Universal Cartographics would be allowed to the table as well. After all, it was all about science, wasn’t it?

No one would destroy the station.

No one would admit publicly how close they had come.

And the Distant Worlds commanders would be pardoned in full and allowed to return home without sanction. The orders rescinding the bounties went out twelve hours later.

The fleet, which had spent those twelve hours on the edge of becoming something far more dangerous than an expedition, settled by degrees.

Not calm.

Not trust.

Something more cautious.

Something like a truce.

---
Later, standing on the outer promenade of Rainbow’s End, Jasmina looked out across the station that should not have existed and yet now shone quietly against the darkness.

Below, somewhere beneath the crust of the lattice it watched over, the rogue AI remained trapped or muffled or slowed. Contained, for now, beneath layers of alloy, mathematics and human bloody-mindedness.

The lattice no longer screamed. It only whispered.

Beside her, Hermine folded her arms and regarded the stars with the expression of a woman already working out how much paperwork all this would generate in peacetime.

“Well,” she said eventually, “that was unpleasant.”

Jasmina laughed softly.

“Yes.”

“You know it isn’t over.”

“No,” Jasmina said. “Only paused. Mare Somnia, a waking beacon in a Sea of Dreams. Let’s hope it’ll not turn into our next nightmare.”

Hermine nodded toward the station lights below.

“Still. Not bad for a glorified data depot.”

Jasmina looked at Rainbow’s End — at the name, the lights, the impossible fact of it — and thought of the Caretakers in their fading harmony, of the many ships that had died trying to say the same thing, of the thing below and the thing beyond and the long chain of accidents and choices that had brought them here.

Contained.

Not ended.

For now, that would have to be enough.

For Michael Brookes.
