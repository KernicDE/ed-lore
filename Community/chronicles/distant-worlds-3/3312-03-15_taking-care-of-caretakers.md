Tianve, 3312 AD

There was an out-of-the-way jewel in the Tianve system. It was an old mining platform at the fringes of the rings of Tianve 9, an otherwise unremarkable icy body in the outer system. The platform had been converted into an observation and entertainment complex by the Tianveian Tourist Board, the TTB.

It was in one of these conference suites the two old men met. While John, the fossil with the blue tinted glasses, sat in one of the yellow lounge sofas, his 'old acquaintance' Cal stood in front of the wall sized crystal window, a tumbler with Lavian Brandy in his hand. He had his back turned to John and was seemingly taking in the glittering, prismatic ring spectacle.

"Fantastic isn't it?" he said to himself. "Tianve of all places. I nearly forgot this place existed. Did you know back in the day people thought there was a pulsar in this system? Those clever TTB marketing bastards."

He then turned to John. "An excellent example of mass manipulation. When the hype was in full swing and the liners were underway they sold all their shares and were never seen again. They knew it would take the liners two weeks to get here. Enough time to liquidate a company and look for greener pastures."

With his legs crossed, John had also chanced a few looks outside. Tianve was still part of the Old Worlds, that ancient conclave of systems that had mostly been settled by early independent souls and mom-and-pop expeditions rather than by industrial heavy weights.

He looked at Cal and just nodded.

"Well," Cal continued. "I understand why you chose this place for a meeting. At least around here nobody wonders about a Lanner flying around with all its parts intact."

"I knew you'd like it," came the eventual retort. "Where heist crimes meet nostalgia."

"Well, if those are the criteria we should have met on Founders World. Or even on Jameson Memorial. The place for the Elite's elite. More fitting, don't you think?"

"John Ermitage," he added. "Ermitage, really? I never took you for a hermit. When the dust settled all these years ago, I always imagined you flying around the galaxy with your newly found friends and freedom, seeing all the places you couldn't in life. Well… in your former life I mean."

"Only it wasn't dust that settled after these actions but ashes," John replied calmly. "Ashes that were the fallout from your shortsightedness."

"The Powers that were begged to differ on quite a few things. But we've been there, John. Done that, got the proverbial T-Shirt. Why have you asked for this meeting? To see me staring at a neutron star that never existed?"

"No, Cal. It's not about nostalgia and it's not about T-Shirts. But funnily it may have something to do with neutron stars and also with things that never existed."

He cocked his head. "I mean, you're the obvious expert on things that never existed, aren't you?"

"No, I'm the expert on making things vanish, John. There's a slight difference. But here we are again, two dinosaurs trading barbs as usual. You can't let go of our old enmity and I fully accept you never will. I was prepared to never see you again. And when you nonetheless came to me last year I told you from the beginning your 'window for cooperation' was a bad idea. Our interests rarely did align."

With that John got up from his couch and walked the few steps towards the crystal window. It was true, it was a magnificent view, pulsar or not.

"Well," he said. "If that's the case then, fine. Still, you seem very enthused to speak with Miss Delarue every once in a while. Maybe the window for cooperation was meant to be not with me but with someone else in the first place?"

He looked at Cal, eyebrows raised — slightly.

"Yes, I enjoy her. She was a good pick. She has a sharp mind and a more general, strategic level of morality that doesn't get in the way all the time because of some minor nuisance. There is still a steep learning curve, to be sure, if you want to play the big game. Having her butt heads with the Dog Star nutters may prove to be a challenge."

"And you?" John asked. "Are you a challenge?"

"Ah," he waved off the question. "It's fine. She asked me a lot about space travel and the galactic North. She obviously doesn't know how space exploration on a big organisational scale worked back then."

"I wonder what you might have told her."

"No, you don't, because you already know. In any case, I did the honest thing and warned her. Sometimes quite bluntly, sometimes with some metaphorical help. I told her humanity should have no business up there."

He paused for a moment. "It… didn't really deter her, I think. Quite the opposite, actually. She seemed especially intrigued by my Umbra Sagittarii reference. Enticed even. There is a very honest curiosity about her, the kind of curiosity that can get you into trouble at times… She reminds me of someone…"

"Yeah, that Umbra of yours never gets old, does it? Space yarn tales."

"You may believe me or not but I was earnest when I warned her. There are regions we shouldn't be in. Not yet. We are over extending. But we keep pushing boundary after boundary just because of 'The Frontier'."

He waved towards the scenery outside the crystal window with his brandy tumbler.

"You know very well, where it got us last time, my friend."

"And who makes the decision when it's time? You say you make things vanish but at the end of the day you deny humanity their path to the stars. And while you may find that phrase amusing it's the truth. Not trying to expand our experience and broaden our horizon means stagnation. You say it's dangerous because you think someone will discover something you guardians of the galaxy can't control."

"Guardians of the galaxy, heh! That's even funnier than all the things they said about Halsey's hallucinations. Or about Patreus' saber rattling how the Pleiades had always been ours to take."

"And no, John. It's more complicated than that and I'm sorry to break it to you but it's about responsibility. It's dangerous in a socio-evolutionary way. Bluntly put, we are progressing too quickly because we think technology and our own grandiose ingenuity allow us; because we can. Then again, governments aren't responsible. They are hasty and greedy, thinking in years or decades at most. Just look at all those lifeless, deserted outposts the Trailblazer Initiative left after a few never-heard-of shell companies started to throw credits around. Is there coherence? No. They are scattering their resources where fancy takes them."

"It's always refreshing seeing you talk about ethics and responsibility," John said. "And I disagree. Human advancement was always driven by exploration and expansion. You can see how the governments' attempts to regulate space exploration have clearly failed. You can't regulate what you can't control."

"I understand the allure but we're not talking about a trek to Oregon or even an outpost chain to the Pleiades. We are talking about space we won't be able to socially and economically curate for a very long time. Our fernweh enthusiasts are now on the verge of colonising the Lagoon Nebula. And for what I ask? Because they have been there and found it a nice place for taking selfies."

"Humanity is squandering resources that are desperately needed elsewhere," Cal finished.

"There are precedents," John said. "Take the Alliance for example. When their technological breakthroughs allowed it, they not only revolutionised space travel but it also ushered in a new era of small scale exploration not seen since the days of GalCop. The return of the mom-and-pop expeditions so to say."

"Argent knew what she was doing and she had acquired a huge stash of GalCop technology. And it only got her to the Corona Australis. But there never was a serious colonisation effort after and people are still searching for her missing Quest ship."

"And Mic Turner? He went to the Pleiades."

Cal smiled again, this time out of pity. "I'm not playing your mind games. I'm too old for that nonsense. And however enlightening it is to discuss galactic matters and humanity's place in all this with you, you once more didn't answer my simple question why you asked for this meeting."

"Very well then. Over the last several months the Distant Worlds expedition has acquired a staggering amount of factual data that hyperspace is somehow bleeding into our known space."

"Factual?" Cal interrupted.

"Yes. It seems especially prevalent close to superdense masses and massive FSD events. Their data suggests a possibly misguided intelligence is behind all this and yes, for the time being they ruled out the Thargoids. It seems to be attuned in some way both to ancient GalCop and Guardian technology and through some presumably old programming seeks to manipulate these for its own unknown gains. There have been a number of crude but corroborating references in the Tionisla archives that mention a 'Caretaker Protocol'."

He looked at Cal. "Ever heard of that before?"

"Caretaker Protocol? That's an interesting name, especially given you say that protocol is apparently not taking care at all."

"You mean you don't know anything about it?"

"I don't, John, as absurd as it may sound to you. I dabbled in a lot of things and know of other things that most people don't. But I'm not omniscient."

"Not anymore at least," he added wryly.

"Halsey said in Tionisla they told her the name came from an old GalCop project report. It must have had something to do with research into machine intelligence meant to control Quirium based FTL drive injections. Coincidentally, ten years ago, Halsey spoke about caretakers in her visions as well."

"It sounds like a very GalCop thing to do, asking a machine to please make FTL work for them. People were fed up with Faraway Jumps and riding high on all kinds of technological wizardry. But again, I don't know anything about caretakers. Yes, research into AI may have been a big thing but after a few catastrophic failures with recycling and environmental control and a few deaths, people decided it was a PR minefield and not worth the money. So they abolished most of it."

"Still, there must be something. You say recycling? They discovered several wrecks in your Archer's Shadow, some of them very old. Every one of them showed signs of matter manipulation on a molecular level. The last hull they found apparently was a RimLiner Anaconda. It can't have gotten there on its own."

"And this is your Caretaker Protocol? A thinking, teleporting recycler gone rogue?"

"I don't know, Cal. That's why I asked for this meeting. To ask you. You of all people still breathing are one of the rare specimens I can ask and expect to get an earnest and reasonable answer; one that isn't always a hundred per cent true but I can accept that."

Cal seemed to consider it all for a few moments and finally leaned against the crystal window. For a moment it looked as if he was falling into the void behind him. He sighed.

"Quirium is a lost case, John. You know that. GalCop collapsed under financial duress and scrutiny, economical and military donors far and wide pulled their cash, Sirius wanted the cake but came too late to the table. And Falayn vanished. Case closed."

"Not closed. We both know some of GalCop survived. People, places, assets… experiments maybe. You mentioned Meredith yourself and we know there were others at the proverbial table, including your 'employers'."

Cal weighed his head. "Maybe, John. But again, nothing of the sort. The governments saw their chance to forbid dabbling into AI after their own research efforts faltered. They didn't want others to succeed where they hadn't. The result was one of the very rare occasions where I would say they were right about it."

"There is something at work here," John mused. "A remnant of something. Not only in the galactic North. An associate of mine linked several puzzle pieces together in an experiment not long ago and was able to reproduce here what has happened tens of thousands of light years away."

"Yes, I know, it was this Ram Tah guy. The Wheel are starting to wonder where all their AI relics went. Soon they'll start looking, I'm afraid. However, it still doesn't explain why hyperspace is involved. And why your something is bleeding into our space."

"Communication?"

"How?"

"We use FTL comms ourselves and we know the Guardians did on a much larger scale. Whatever is out there allegedly uses — or rather usurps — their channels, so to speak. But it all seems to result in a jumble. We have heard distorted comms from ships that went missing ages ago. It's almost as if hyperspace was capable of storing all these bits and pieces."

"Well, guess what now, John! You're the one who had the brilliant idea to 'borrow' the sensor array of the Sarasvati, an old GalCop listening post. See the irony here? A listening post doing its job by listening and reporting? What were you thinking that old gizmo was going to do? Read the Galnet news to you?"

"We certainly didn't anticipate receiving comms messages from the Tau Ceti colonisation, the Birthright Wars, the Republic of Achenar or some other event hundreds of years ago."

"Seriously, why do you think people think witch-space is spooky and malign?"

"What if it's not witch-space that is malign but rather what may be residing inside?"

"I'll leave that to your speculating friends out there. And if your brilliant astronomer is right and hyperspace is indeed a zero time medium, information being contained there indefinitely isn't that far fetched. Maybe we're just lacking the correct receiver at the moment. I mean, look at how the cosmic background radiation was dicovered and later led to early astronomical breakthroughs."

"Like I said earlier," he mused. "Sometimes we are just not meant to have the technology for the next step. It's called the progress trap. After a period of rapid advancement and increasing social and economic complexity, any civilisation needs a revolutionary technological breakthrough in order to advance further. If they don't achieve it they will eventually vanish in a self-destructive struggle over resources. Again, I leave the interpretation to you and your friends."

"This doesn't sound like the man who thought he had the tools to play god."

"Because I never was that man, John. None of us were. For all your bitterness and your theories on caretaking, we were probably the next best analogy."

"And you're still doing it now?"

"Yes, oddly. However, there isn't much I can do right now. Your expedition is thousands of light years away, your little coterie of devotees is fractured, your senator is dead and the Wheel keeps turning, to borrow a phrase. And I told Delarue everything I could about 'ye olden days'. For what it's worth, she asked me to keep tabs on some potential backstabbers here in our very civilised space. You know, the usual suspects who want to cash in on a quick political win. But they are all freaking out about blue gardens now."

"Blue gardens?" John asked

"Yes, a Radicoida specimen. They are bioluminescent, hence the 'blue' pun. A very promising find. They are also a minor interest of mine."

"You don't have minor interests, Cal. That's why I called for this meeting."
