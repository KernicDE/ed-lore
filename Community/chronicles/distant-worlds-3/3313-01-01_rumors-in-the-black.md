Rendezvous Point, 3313 AD, Supercruise

The flotilla slid into formation like a school of bright fish. Wake geometry braided thin so as not to rattle the stragglers. Ahead, the primary star burned a comfortable white, its chart label on every canopy simply reading the bookmarked location: Rendezvous Point.

“Area sweep as per plan,” Captain Yuen said. “Wide cone, wake net at twenty-kilometre intervals. Keep your scanners sharp. The search is underway.”

Halsey watched the traffic bloom on the tactical. Caspian hulls peeled off in pairs, independents filled the gaps. She felt the tug in her ribs soften to the background. Quieter here. Nothing like what she had sensed before.

She raised an eyebrow at that and then looked over to the captain. “How soon will we know?”

“Depends on whether there’s anything to find,” the captain murmured.

For many hours, nothing came back. No signs, no anomalies, no signal sources worthy more than a cursory examination. Jasmina helped herself to a coffee and then called up the holofac transmitters.

Timotheus Haddard, the Distant Sun’s quartermaster, came in on the logistics net, less grin than usual. “DSSA reps on our carriers have been helpful to a fault but none can find the callsign for this carrier. I am getting shrugs and polite bafflement. That is not a combination I enjoy.”

“I can help with that,” Hermine said. “My contacts back in the bubble have just told me our mystery ship is the Universal Cartographics cruiser, Guinevere. Eridanus-class. Two-year assignment filed with the Pilots’ Federation Logistics Board. Survey scope: K-type primaries, high probability of undiscovered terraformables and pristine rings.”

“Sounds right,” Jasmina said, with a nod. “That tallies with what I know. Does it help us find it?”

“Not really,” Timotheus replied. “But it will help narrow things down if we do encounter a wreck or a beacon.”

“Can we narrow the search area somehow?” Hermine asked.

Jasmina thought for a moment.

“How about our lead astrophysicist?” she asked. “Lya might have…”

Hermine rolled her eyes.

“Really? The lead astrophysicist who hates space?”

“I admit she’s unconventional,” Jasmina replied. “But space is big… we need to narrow our focus…”

“Theory never beats practical experience,” Hermine said. “It’s all very well sitting back home in the safety of your nice warm ivory tower while real explorers take the actual risks…”

“I think she can help us,” Jasmina said, softly.

Hermine shrugged. Jasmina keyed the holofac and another figure joined the telepresence.

The wall-sized holo on the bridge flickered to a woman in her early thirties with bright blonde hair in a disciplined bun and the expression of someone who really objected to any kind of face to face communication.

“Lya,” Jasmina said. “We’re hooked up with Timotheus and Hermine.”

“Awesome! The TelePros seem to be aligned just fine!” Lya said. “Lovely to see you too, Timetheus.” She paused. “Oh, and Hermine I see. All well?”

Hermine narrowed her eyes. Timotheus shifted from side to side.

“So far so good,” Jasmina said. “We’re hoping you can help us.”

Jasmina quickly brought her up to speed.

“Lya, your floor. Anything you’ve got would be helpful.”

“Splendid! And thanks for thinking of me from so far away!” Lya said. “Well, if the Guinevere was on a K-type survey mandate then she’ll have left fingerprints. K-primaries sit in a sweet spot for water worlds and ELWs. Cartographics cruisers do not just honk and go. They park. They probe. They leave away teams and drop scanner packages. That’s UniCart 101. They run multi-band probes on the rings and they drape a net of beacon pings across the whole place. There will be some evidence of where they are and where they went.”

She blinked at someone off camera and then nodded before turning back to the holofac. “I’d suggest doing something similar out there. You need to fly in your wing thingies and survey K-type stars within one jump of your current location. Or two… Or make it three. Run discovery scans but prioritise space signals and non-standard broadbands. Wake scanners in passive mode. If you have any Guardian wake enhancements, leave them bolted on to the FSS. K-types are EM noisy but they are honest. You’ll find a clue, I promise.”

Jasmina nodded. Timotheus looked optimistic. Hermine looked skeptical.

“We’ll try,” Jasmina said. ‘Thankyou.”

“Most welcome,” Lya said. “Oh, and relay me all their astro spreadsheets if you find any. Ivory tower, out.”

The holofac faded. Timotheus stifled a laugh.

Jasmina smiled and then gestured to the captain.

Captain Yuen nodded. “We’ll break the fleet into three sweeps. Leave it with me and Howard.”

---
They found the beacon on the second day after arrival.

Nor — a pilot from Colonia — was the first to catch it. “Science, I have a broadband squawk at 12.2 kHz above the soup. Wide and ugly. And odd. Modulation looks distressed. Origin near the ecliptic.”

“Mark and hold,” Hermine said. “Do not touch. Fleet, we have a live one. Passive only.”

Captain Yuen bled the engines down to a sensible speed. The K-type primary filled half the canopy, an amber patience. Off to port a freckle of manufactured light blinked, wrong in cadence and colour.

“Range ten,” the helm called. “Scoop deployed. No grav anomalies.”

“Nice and smooth. Putting it in the cargo bay,” comms said.

There was a faint clunk from somewhere below the bridge.

“Let’s go,” Jasmina said, heading towards the rear.

The beacon was nothing the team anticipated. It was more akin to a satellite, ancient and comparably crude. Its casing had been patched with what looked like duct tape and angry welding. This was neither an emergency beacon nor was it contemporary ship equipment in any way. The paint on the side had once been fashionably orange. Someone had stencilled a half visible and discomforting “DSSA” with an Arc Cutter on its metal belly. 

Jasmina’s stomach clenched. “This…,” she pointed at the satellite. “I have seen this before. I just…”

“Wideband,” the engineer interrupted when he got a chance to inspect it in the cargo bay. “No handshakes. No key. Badly damaged, but I think I can… yeah, here we go…”

The beacon suddenly spoke in bursts. A dead man’s voiceline, stitched and stretched.

The cargo bay went quiet in that way ships do when everyone is listening to the same ghost.

“Again,” Jasmina said, softer. “Can you augment the audio at all?”

They let it roll until the clock said it had repeated three times exactly, but it was no clearer. The captain lifted a hand. “Take a break. Science has the raw. I’ll make a note to thank CMDR Nor, that was a lucky find.”

“I’ll pull the casing,” the engineer said. “Might be able to salvage something more from it.”

Jasmina nodded absently.

The camera drone slid close and took the kind of images administrators love. Scoring on the data plate. A fleet maintenance sticker, six months old. The DSSA crest stamped off-center by someone having a bad day.

“Any locator?” Hermine asked.

“Nope,” the engineer said. “But the configuration is odd. I mean steel and titanium plating? Hello 34th century? A lot of damage here, understandably, but some of the data core is intact so at least the shielding held. But again, no proto or radiolytic alloys? Who built this thing? And more importantly, when? But we’ll probably need more compute power than we can summon out here in the void.”

“Extract what you can,” Jasmina said. “We’ll send it to Lya and see what she can make of it.”

Hermine sighed. “So she can save the day again from the comfort of her armchair.”

---
Back in the bubble Lya had wasted no time. Analysts in fleet jumpsuits and borrowed lab coats fed the beacon data into a dozen open mouths of software and let them chew.

“Spectral first,” Lya said. “The beacon’s wideband is trash but trash has fingerprints. Look for sidebands no one asked for. Look for a carrier…” She chuckled. “...on the carrier.”

Numbers stacked, data flowed, screens plotted graphs. An analyst raised a hand. “There is a comb at forty-two hertz riding the entire packet. Six teeth. Equal spacing. Could be chassis vibration, could be an intentional watermark.”

“Could be a wake bleed,” someone else said. “Fifteen shows up faint.”

“Note it, but do not fall in love with it,” Lya said. “Temporal next.”

They pinned the repeats in time and found a micro-drift where none should have been. The kind of drift you get when your clock comes from the sky and the sky has been bent.

“Plot this on the galactic chart,” Lya said. “If the beacon floated a while before it got tired and started yelling then solar wind should have pushed it. Solve for deployment point by back-tracing on the minor outgassing we can hear in the noise.”

Lines darted, then erased, then came back thicker. A ghost-trajectory painted itself across the holofac. It did not point back to the Rendezvous Point. It curled spinwise and up, toward a pane of the map that had always been a polite blank.

“Again,” Lya said. “Assume the deployment was deliberate. Weighted throw calculations. Give me a cone, not a line.”

The cone grew, faint and infuriating. At its thin end a cluster of systems lit up in pale triangles. All of them wore the same quiet label every pilot knows and then pretends not to be interested in.

Permit-locked.

“Well, well, well… Curiouser and curiouser,” Lya breathed.

---
Later that day Lya made her report.

Hermine drew a breath. “Names?”

The system designations scrolled past like a list you only see when you have done something to earn a look. Among them, a map name floated up from the archives like a fish coming to bread.

“Praei2,” Lya read. “Someone had a laconic week. It’s a permit-locked sector in the northern pane.”

Hermine looked alarmed for a moment. Jasmina felt the tug in her ribs tap once, as if in approval.

Yes, this is it…

Flight Major Elaine Howard broke the silence before it got ideas. “So. A UC cruiser on a K-type mandate. Fine. A DSSA carrier we cannot name. An ages old beacon built in a hurry that says Guinevere ran into big trouble in the worst accent I have ever heard. And our cone points at a sector I cannot buy a permit for.”

“Why does a DSSA carrier drop a beacon and carry on as if it were just another normal day,” Captain Yuen asked the room at large. “Someone should be screaming. Yet DSSA says no knowledge.”

“Either the DSSA carrier was not DSSA,” Hermine said, “or someone has asked or paid very nicely for silence.”

“Or both,” Lya said. “The maths does not mind which of your politics you choose.”

Jasmina looked at the clean cone, then at the ugly satellite, then at the sealed case by her chair. The Quirium unit was quiet.

“We need a name,” she said. “If a carrier was in that pane and not on any DSSA ledger then someone filed it somewhere else. Hermine, what’s up?”

Hermine still looked uncomfortable.

“There is a person,” she said. “An associate who talks to me when I invite him to ‘94 Lavian Brandy. He calls himself Cal because he likes being difficult. He collects myths about early hyperspace work and calls them safety lectures. Last time we spoke he warned me off the northern pane and how it spooked early explorers. He called it the Umbra Sagittarii like he wanted the words to stick in my ears. The Archer’s Shadow. It sounded strange that he made that remark at that time.”

“He also called Deep Space Surveys a ‘little NGO’,” she added sourly.

“He knows something about this region?” Howard asked.

“Maybe, maybe not,” Hermine replied.

“Do you trust him?” Captain Yuen asked.

“I trust that he believes he is right,” Hermine said. “And that he answers questions with a different question unless you trap him in a corner.”

“So trap him,” Lya said, cheerfully unhelpful. “Rouse him with jam and judicious advice. Give him conundrums to guess.”

All the others turned their heads towards the TelePro in unusual simultaneity. 

“What?” Lya protested. “No one said scientists can’t be poets! We’re hunting a Snark here, aren’t we?”

Jasmina nodded once, irritation still written on her face. “Well, worth a shot, poetry or not. Ask your Cal. Tell him we have a dead cruiser’s whisper and a route that ends in a permit lock. If he wants to keep us out he can explain why to the authorities. Remind him I have a direct line to the Federation, Empire and the Alliance. That should be enough of a trap to motivate him.”

Hermine’s silence contained movement, the kind made by fingers calling in favours. “On it,” she said. “But I can’t promise.”

“I know,” Jasmina said.

Hermine turned to the captain. “In the meantime keep the fleet on the published scaffold. Survey Refinement Alpha continues. Quiet eyes on K-types along the cone. No heroics. If a DSSA carrier is out there under a different flag then it is either bait or a friend. I would prefer to know which before we say hello.”

“Understood,” the captain said. “I’ll work on something plausible with logistics.”

On the far wall the cone hung like a pale spear pointed into a map that refused to be helpful. Jasmina felt the seam in her bones again, the miss on fifteen that had followed them since Mars. Out here it sounded like something tapping at a locked door with more patience than she wanted to imagine.

Jasmina looked out at the K-type stars plot. The ghosts were learning to write. The missing carrier had to be the blank page ready for the words. Now she needed someone to tell her what was being written.
