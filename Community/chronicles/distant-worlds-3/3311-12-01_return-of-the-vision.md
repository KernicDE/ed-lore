Sol, Mars, 3311 AD

The lights of the auditorium were a soft Martian amber, the sort chosen to flatter skin tones, hide wrinkles and disguise fatigue. Jasmina Halsey steadied herself at the lectern, one hand on the frosted glass, the other closing around the edge of her commtab. She had delivered harder addresses in harsher rooms. This one should have been easy: a few measured remarks about cooperative research, a nod to Alliance liaison, polite applause, then biscuits and strong coffee.

The first wobble came in the second paragraph. Not in her voice — in her mind.

A pressure, as intimate as breath but coming from everywhere at once, passed through the floor into her bones. It was almost music, a low mechanical throb just below hearing, as if the hall’s ventilation had learned to count and then forgotten how. The text on her commtab blurred; her mouth raced to keep pace with a sentence that had become unintelligible. Someone in the front row frowned. Someone else stood.

“—to recognise that what unites our—”

The pressure increased. The world tilted. Stars, a strange tone, a sense of urgency. Witch-space? Witch-space!

The last thing she remembered was the glass rim of a water tumbler, ringing like a struck crystal as she fell, the droplets hanging in slow motion.

---
White light, then soft dark. Headache. A slow return through layers of sound: a medevac’s inertial hum, a stretcher’s rattle. Her sister? Yes — Azalea’s voice pitched low and furious at a nurse who deserved neither. Smells arranged themselves — antiseptic, warm steel, the faint tang of recycled air cooled too fast.

“Jas? Jas!” Azalea’s hand enclosed hers. “You’re in Olympus Village Medical. You gave us a fright.”

Jasmina opened her eyes. Her sister’s face swam into focus, framed by the discreet logo of the facility on the glass partition. A Federal Navy duty officer glanced in from the corridor and tactfully withdrew.

“How long?” Jasmina asked.

“Hours,” Azalea said. “Not days. They say your vitals stabilised in transit.”

A physician appeared with the practiced quiet of people who belong in ICUs. “Madam. Dr Leto — neurology. The scan shows no stroke, no embolism, nothing catastrophic. Temporary hypotension and syncope, exacerbated by stress. No need to worry.”

Behind the doctor’s words the pulse had returned, a strange tone, faint but insistent, lurking on the edge of hearing.

“I didn’t faint,” she said, with sudden clarity. “I was listening.”

Azalea’s hand tightened. “To what?”

Jasmina turned her head toward the ceiling, as if looking might help her hear. “A rhythm. Out of true. Witch-space… And the voices — the ones that called themselves Caretakers.”

The temperature in the room seemed to drop. Azalea’s body stiffened; her expression hardened.

The doctor pursed his lips. “You have a history of—”

“Of seeing too far,” Jasmina said gently, her voice soft and dreamy. “Yes. 3302. Gardens and horizons and songs people kindly labelled delusion. This was clearer. Not an invitation this time, but a warning.”

Azalea caught the physician’s eye. “May I have a moment alone with my sister, please?”

He withdrew. When they were alone, Jasmina closed her fingers, as if the lectern were still under her hand.

“Jas,” Azalea whispered. “Don’t do this again… please…”

“You don’t understand,” Jasmina replied. “It’s not me… it’s them.”

Azalea sighed, rubbed her forehead, then pulled up a chair and sat alongside the medical couch. “All right. What did you see?”

“I stood in a darkness that should have been empty,” Jasmina said, straining for the right words. “But I could feel a fabric under tension. There was a pulse running through it, like a jump ship’s throb slipping a cog every so often. And the Caretakers—” She paused, tasting the fear. “They were afraid. Something in witch-space is wrong. That’s what they said.”

Azalea pressed her fingers to her brow. “Are you absolutely sure? You know what happens if this all starts again. The questions… the smears… the lies…”

“I know. But this is more important than just me.”

Silence, punctuated only by the intermittent beeps of the medical tech.

“Then we treat it as real until proven otherwise,” Azalea said at last.

Jasmina chuckled. Azalea had always been the practical one.

“Anyway,” Azalea continued, “we’ve no time to debate. Felicia’s office has already called. She wants to check on you personally — she’s waiting outside. And that old fossil Delaney has just landed too.”

“Felicia Winters?” Jasmina said. “Of course. She will also want me to behave.”

“She wants you alive.”

“You’d better send her in, then.”

The partition cleared with a diplomatic chime. Two security staff took up positions just outside as Felicia Winters stepped in with the measured stride of a woman accustomed to both crisis and cameras. She dismissed the guards with a nod.

“Jasmina,” Felicia said, and the formality dropped from her voice. “You scared the hell out of a very expensive room full of very irritating people asking a lot of very good questions.”

“I’ve grown theatrical in my old age,” Jasmina managed, smiling.

“Is it…?” Felicia asked.

Azalea was already nodding.

“Felicia, I heard something. Not metaphor, not poetry. A pulse. It felt like the void itself was torn, twisted… hurting.”

Felicia considered that for a long moment. “You know I have to ask: are you certain you’re not… hallucinating?”

“It’s clearer this time,” Halsey said. “The Caretakers again — but not announcing themselves. Warning. And there was a sound. If you have the auditorium’s environmental recordings, ask an engineer to pull the subsonic. It will be there. I know it.”

Felicia exhaled through her nose. “You do realise your detractors will have a field day with this… and mine too.”

“They made a decade of the last one,” Halsey said. “I survived. You’ve done all right, too.”

Felicia’s gaze flicked to Azalea, who answered with a small, stubborn lift of her chin. “I saw the GalNet leak,” Felicia said. “Nicely worded. Patient confidentiality. Summit proceeds. Ambulances and assurances. Azalea, I see your fingerprints on the bit about the Imperial Ambassador.”

“Just protocol,” Azalea said primly. “Her medical indemnity pulls in an Embassy witness. It’s duller than it sounds.”

“Nothing about you two is ever dull,” Felicia said, some of the steel leaving her tone. She pulled a chair closer, sat so they were eye to eye. “Very well. Here’s what I can offer without feeding the trolls. I’ll grant you a private leave of absence. Off the public roster, one secure update to my office every seventy-two hours. You will not travel alone. You will not make public pronouncements without warning me first. In return, you pursue this quietly and with rigour. If there’s a real signal in there, we’ll find it. If it’s just your health talking, we’ll find that, too.”

Relief loosened Jasmina’s shoulders. “Thank you.”

Felicia’s smile was tired and genuine. “You’ve earned the right to call in a few favours. Use them well.”

A discreet buzz at the door. An aide leaned in. “Madam President? Confirmation that Ambassador Delaney has arrived. The Embassy memo describes his presence as ‘supportive and in-line with established protocol.’”

Felicia’s eyebrows rose a fraction. “The usual decorum. Fine. Keep the cameras away from the lifts and bring him up.”

The aide withdrew. Felicia stood, smoothing an imaginary crease. “So, where do you need to go, then?”

The question hung there, crisp as a Martian night.

Before Jasmina could answer, the partition chimed again. An attaché in Imperial livery appeared at the threshold, all poise and immaculate cuffs.

“Ambassador Delaney,” he announced softly.

“That was damn quick,” Felicia muttered.

Cuthrick Delaney entered with the careful dignity of a man who refused to hurry for anything short of an absolute emergency. He was older than the last time Jasmina had seen him; the years had added gravitas to the eyes and a courtier’s stoop to the shoulders. But the gaze was steady, and there was a seasoned habit in the way he took the room’s bearings: door, exits, monitors, herself and the other occupants.

“Dear Jasmina, Azalea — a delight as always,” he said, warm and accommodating. Then, with a nod to Felicia and a crisper tone: “Madam President.”

Felicia’s mouth twitched. “Ambassador. Since protocol insists upon your company, do make yourself useful.”

His smile was faint and genuine. “I shall try — though that would be a novelty.”

He drew up a third chair — not too close — and folded himself into it. “The Caretakers, I assume?”

“This time it was a warning,” Jasmina replied. “A strange tone… and a word… witch-space.”

“Mm. Not a term many use nowadays.” He glanced towards the ceiling, as though listening to a ship hum that wasn’t there. “My grandfather kept a dog-eared primer of those old spacer terms in an actual book. Imagine that… of course he never…”

“You’re not here to reminisce,” Felicia snapped. “Do you know what it means?”

“No,” Delaney said, “but perhaps I once did. It may come to me. In the meantime: if the Caretakers are pointing at something the present prefers to call ‘ancient history’, consulting an historian would seem to be the obvious solution. There’s one place where that knowledge is still maintained without being… edited to comply with the tastes of modern audiences.”

He paused, folded his arms and waited a moment until all eyes were on him. “Tionisla.”

Azalea made a small, sceptical noise. Felicia’s brows went up a millimetre. “The Tionisla Historical Society? Eccentrics and conspiracy theorists. And it’s Alliance space.”

“How inconvenient for suspicion,” Delaney returned mildly. “Yet they are the best historical society left standing. The curators are tediously incorruptible – let me assure you, we’ve tried! I’ll admit they’re mildly eccentric. Their memorialists gather more data than anyone alive. If there’s a clue to be found, that’s where I’d look first.”

Felicia folded her arms. “You’re asking me to send a former Federal president — fresh out of intensive care — into an Alliance system, on the advice of an Imperial ambassador, to consult a bunch of… eccentrics… about ancient history.”

“Admirably succinct,” Delaney said with a grin. “We know you’ve pulled the auditorium logs already; our network of…”

“Spies?” Felicia asked, with just the right amount of acid in her tone.

“Facilitators,” Cuthrick replied smoothly. “Have provided us with a copy. Your engineers will find a notch at fifteen cycles and a harmonic smear in the mid-range. They will write ‘interference’ on the report because that is what people write in reports about things that don’t have a proper explanation.”

Azalea’s eyes flicked to Felicia. “We have asked for the recording.”

Felicia didn’t look away, continuing to glare at Cuthrick. “And if the analysis matches?”

“Then the venue isn’t the only thing ringing,” Cuthrick said. “Which suggests the message was directed — at her.”

He nodded to Jasmina.

Silence settled, purposeful. Jasmina felt the old rhythm tap once at the edge of hearing. She looked at Felicia. “If there’s a thread to pull, I have to go to Tionisla.”

Felicia looked around at the group. Then she sighed, the sound of a politician deciding which lie would hurt less.

“Very well. If we do this, we do it with choreography. Medical cover, humanitarian language. The Alliance is briefed through the Office of the Prime Minister; they issue the invitation so I can pretend I’m merely acquiescing. You—” she aimed a finger at Cuthrick “—keep your banners folded. No triumphalism in my house or theirs.”

“I am very rarely triumphant nowadays,” Cuthrick said. “More’s the pity.”

Azalea cut in, practical as gravity. “We’ll need a quiet corridor out of Olympus. No cameras, no long lenses.”

Felicia nodded. “I’ll arrange a diplomatic maintenance window on the east spine. Thirty minutes. If anyone complains, we’ll call it a filter change.” She softened, just a fraction, to Jasmina. “You are not to make public statements en route. If asked, you are resting.”

“Resting will be easy enough.”

Cuthrick rose, joints protesting in the small way old joints do. “There’s a curator at the Tionisla Historical Society — Dr Reuben Hale. He is—” Cuthrick searched for the word “—unfashionably precise. He won’t try to believe you. He’ll try to measure you. That’s probably what you need.”

Felicia arched an eyebrow. “You know him?”

“I was young once, believe it or not,” Cuthrick said, “and impertinent about words like witch-space. He corrected me. I didn’t enjoy the experience, but I did learn from it.”

Jasmina smiled at the idea of Cuthrick being dressed down by… anyone.

Azalea shook her head. “I’m not thrilled about handing Jasmina to Alliance nutters…”

“You’re handing her to a man who labels things with fastidious attention to detail,” Delaney said, unruffled. “The alternatives are redacted Federal or Imperial files. Our organisations are famously good at not revealing anything useful.”

Felicia snorted. “We can agree on that at least. The federation will produce four committees and an empty binder after months of so-called research.”

“Quite,” Cuthrick said. “Tionisla it is, then.”

Felicia looked to Jasmina one last time, seeking doubt and finding none. “You’ll have the leave I promised. Seventy-two-hour updates, secure comms only. If the Alliance so much as smiles oddly, I will personally drag you back to Mars and declare you allergic to history.”

“Understood.”

Felicia extended a hand; Jasmina took it. The grip was warm, firm, and brief. “Get some sleep. Wheels up at oh-six under humanitarian routing. My office will inform Mahon’s people this is a recuperative visit at their invitation.” She eyed Cuthrick. “Ambassador, if you turn this into an Imperial photo opportunity, I will have you installed on a Martian subcommittee on air-quality standards and lose the key. Clear?”

Cuthrick bowed, just enough. “As crystal, Madam President. It would be a fate worse than death indeed.” He paused at the door. “By the way… Ask your engineers to check for a phase miscount in the recording. Today’s technicians mistake it for wear and tear. Dr Hale may have a different opinion.”

Felicia blinked, filed it away, pushed past Cuthrick and departed in a rustle of orders.

Azalea exhaled, long and low. “You do realise we’re about to start a very polite argument with half the galaxy.”

Jasmina let her head sink back into the pillow. The tone returned — tick, miss, resume — like a vast machine slipping a tooth. “Then let’s argue in the right library.”

At the threshold, Cuthrick gave her a look containing something like respect, or recognition. “Tionisla will have the answers,” he said. “And history is very good at keeping quiet while you read.”

The door sighed shut. The room shrank to glass, monitors, and soft Martian air. Azalea fussed with the blanket until Jasmina caught her hand and held it still.

“Get the recording from the summit,” Jasmina murmured. “If it sings the same note, we’re not chasing ghosts.”

Azalea nodded. “I’ll find someone who still enjoys arguing with Fourier transforms. Damn those smug Imperials!”

Jasmina closed her eyes. The rhythm waited, steady and wrong. Beyond the window, Olympus Village eased toward evening.

Tionisla, she thought. Old words, older machines, and a man who labels things properly.
