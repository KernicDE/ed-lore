Tionisla, 3311 AD

The annex smelled faintly of old metals and preservation oils. Display plinths held relics from a less cautious age: battered jump-beacons, scorched drive coils, a flight recorder whose housing had bubbled like toffee under heat. Holograms drifted above each piece, annotating manufacturers long since defunct: Cowell & MgRath, Kruger, Irrikon, Lance & Ferman — even the GalCop Standards Bureau.

Jasmina Halsey walked between them with Azalea a soft presence at her shoulder.&nbsp;

Ambassador Cuthrick Delaney kept pace a few steps back, hands folded over his cane, the picture of diplomatic restraint. Public interest had surged the moment her arrival leaked; the main galleries thrummed with visitors and local press but the annex was quiet by design. A single glass wall looked out toward the Tionisla Orbital Graveyard, its glittering procession of sarcophagi sliding past on patient vectors.

Dr Reuben Hale — thin, grey-eyed, hands permanently smudged with the ghost of workshop grit — waited by a long worktable. He didn’t offer a handshake; he inclined his head, pursing his lips at the delay.

“Madam President.”

“Jasmina, please,” she said. “Titles make people stare.”

“They were staring already,” Hale replied, not unkindly. His gaze flicked to Delaney. “Ambassador.”

“Dr Hale,” Cuthrick said, with the cordiality of old sparring partners. “Still labelling things properly, I trust.”

“Someone must retain standards,” Hale murmured. He gestured to three objects laid out beneath a sheen of stasis-film. “Before we talk about visions, we should agree on the terms of our discussion.”

Halsey glanced at Cuthrick; he returned an I told you so look.

“Ancient hyperspace physics,” Halsey said. “Witch-space if you prefer the older word. I know I do.”

Hale’s mouth twitched. “Then a brisk tour is required.”

He drew breath and launched into a rapid-fire précis, clearly recited often.

“The story begins in the twenty-third century: primitive hyperspace jump engines, high casualty rates. It took six hundred years to even partially master the technique. The first privateer vessels began explorations in the 2800s, following construction of the Faraway system.”

“Far… away?” Cuthrick asked. “Very poetic.”

“Indeed,” Hale said. “Also complex, inefficient and ruinously expensive. A lattice of monitoring satellites, branch lines, stop points and rescue stations — all built at sublight along the major routes. Hundreds of channels in the end. It took decades.”

“So… you had to travel to the destination at sublight to set up the route?” Azalea queried.

“Precisely.”

“No wonder it took decades,” Jasmina said, nodding.

“It’s from this period that your term ‘witch-space’ originates,” Hale continued. “Back then, hyperspacing ships required external help to initiate the jump. The system was… sensitive. Misconfiguration risks are listed as ‘atomic reorganisation’ and ‘time displacement’.”

“I imagine that would have quite the egregious effect on the constitution,” Cuthrick volunteered.

“Quite,” Hale said, with an irritated glare. “Unsupervised jumps were extremely dangerous. Many ships were lost. Travellers believed hyperspace was haunted — ‘the ghosts of the early ships that went into Faraway and didn’t come out again’ — hence witch-space. A large number of ships never arrived; their fate remains unknown.”

“I’m surprised anyone travelled at all,” Azalea said.

“It was a different time,” Hale allowed, a thread of nostalgia there. “More primitive but more daring.”

“And then?” Jasmina prompted.

“Technological progression made Faraway obsolete by the thirty-second century,” Hale said. “A fascinating detail: the discovery of Quirium.”

“Quirium?” Azalea asked.

“A wonder fuel,” Hale said, smiling. “Powerful, stable, contained — the foundation on which an entire superpower rose and fell. You’ve heard of GalCop of course.”

Jasmina and Azalea nodded.

“It powered everything and it revolutionised hyperspace travel,” Hale went on. “No more Faraway lattice. A modern experience we’d recognise today: crossing light-years in moments — magical.”

“But GalCop collapsed,” Azalea said. “If I remember my edu-classes correctly.”

“Yes,” Hale said, cutting a glare towards Cuthrick. “In 3174 GalCop ceased to be. You can blame our Imperial friends.”

“I’m not sure you can lay the collapse entirely at the Empire’s door,” Cuthrick replied, wryly amused.

“I could make a persuasive attempt,” Hale scowled. “Regardless, it fell — and Quirium’s secret fell with it. Lost. A tragedy. Federal and Imperial hyperspace science lagged by decades — centuries even. Weeks again to travel between systems… until very recently.”

“The frameshift drive,” Jasmina whispered.

Azalea folded her arms. “We rebuilt the ladder without the original rungs.”

“Nicely put,” Hale agreed. “And somewhere between the rungs, people fell. In the annals we call them the Missing.”

Jasmina looked up. “The Missing?”

“Our collective term for vessels that entered hyperspace throughout history and did not return,” Hale said. “For the most part it’s incomplete logs, corrupted beacons, survivor testimony that doesn’t survive peer review. You’ll find the generation ships in the list, it’s a complete menagerie of vessels in fact. And yet, occasionally there are patterns, curious anomalies…”

Jasmina shivered. “Like a faint signal from…”

“Ghosts?” Cuthrick offered, then softened it with a small smile. “Or the sort of coincidences that committees redact?”

“Residuals,” Hale corrected briskly. “Noise with structure. I’m convinced it’s some kind of signal. But we’ve never been able to isolate it. Simply not enough actual data.”

Halsey’s gaze drifted to the glass wall, to the slow ballet of the Graveyard beyond. “I heard them in my dreams long before the doctors taught me the phrase ‘auditory hallucination’. Like a pulse under everything.”

“A pulse.” Hale tapped the table. “I’ve reviewed your auditorium recording. It contains traces of the same cadence. I’m convinced it’s not a hallucination — but a fingerprint. A witch-space signature if you like.”

Azalea leaned closer. “We’ve discussed terms, Doctor. What can you tell us about the origin of this signal? What does it mean?”

“Patience,” he said. “There is more you need to know first.”

Azalea glared; Jasmina lifted a placating hand.

Hale slid aside the stasis-film. The first object was a GalCop flight ops ledger — paper, actual paper — its entries ruled in violet ink. The second was a data wafer in an archaic format. The third was a hand-sized polyhedral lump of matte ceramic and gold mesh with three recessed sockets and an inset plaque: GCConSpec — Quirium Control Unit — Mk III.

Hale touched the ledger with a reverent finger. “This first artefact. It’s a thirty-first-century procurement note which reads: Project Caretaker — budget line transfer to Standards; remit: preventative containment system for emergent machine intelligence. Status: unknown.”

“Caretaker?” Jasmina said, eyes widening. “That can’t be a coincidence!”

“I suspect not,’ Hale agreed.

“I thought only I had heard that word… seen those visions,” Jasmina added.

“It would appear,” Cuthrick said, “that someone — perhaps many someones — were familiar with this in the GalCop era. More than two hundred years ago.”

“And history has forgotten it,” Azalea said, looking at her sister. “Until they contacted you.”

“It mentions emergent machine intelligence,” Jasmina repeated with distaste. “You mean sentient AIs?”

“The wording is elliptical,” Hale said. “As you’re aware, such things were banned by treaty — but crucially not until after GalCop’s collapse.”

“GalCop were using sentient AI?” Jasmina asked, horrified.

“It’s not entirely clear,” Hale replied. “Let’s say certain programmes were renamed, relocated or… forgot themselves. But the word ‘Caretaker’ appears here and is also stamped on the reverse meta of this second artefact – the data wafer. The file is mostly corrupted. What remains suggests a supervisory intelligence capability linked to Quirium control hardware — something intended to monitor, shape or restrain behaviour at the interface between drive systems and navigation.”

“So in the absence of the Faraway system,” Azalea said, “they needed something to provide navigational expertise… and they used AI?”

Hale looked up, impressed. “I think it’s a reasonable conclusion. The Faraway system was dismantled by this point… well, mostly. Much of it was simply abandoned in the depths of space, not economical to retrieve.”

“Sentient AI,” Jasmina whispered, her gaze unfocused.

“This third artefact is a Quirium Control Unit, it was standard issue for late-GalCop witch-drives,” Hale added. “This one is inert — or should be. I’ve studied hundreds. Most are merely dead weight curiosities without the rest of their supporting componentry. We’ve never been able to figure out how they worked.”

Jasmina’s attention narrowed to the unit. The hairs lifted along her forearms; an old, unwelcome familiarity, like recognising a lullaby in static.

“May I?”

Hale hesitated, then nodded. “Of course.”

She reached out to the control unit. Her fingertips found the cool planes, the grippy little ridges where techs’ gloves once needed purchase. For a heartbeat nothing happened.

Then the room closed about her, echoing, shuddering, pulsing.

It was subtle and unmistakable: a low, mechanical throb that wasn’t sound so much as pressure, as if the deck had become a diaphragm and chosen delicately to breathe. Azalea’s eyes flicked to Hale; Cuthrick’s hand tightened on his cane. Halsey kept her palm where it was because the pulse carried a cadence she had already learned to dread — and to follow.

“Out of true,” she whispered. “One miss in every so many.”

Hale had gone very still. He didn’t move to instruments; he watched her, not the relic. “You can sense the phase,” he said softly. “We’ve caught that rhythm in the lab only once and labelled it as environmental interference — so we told ourselves.”

Jasmina released her grip on the unit. The pressure receded as if a tide had slipped under the floor and out into the station’s bones.

Azalea let out a breath she hadn’t meant to hold. “I’ll ask again… what does it mean?”

Hale’s professional detachment frayed into awe. “Madam Presi— Jasmina,” he corrected. “We catalogue phenomena. We do not normally… experience them. You spoke of voices calling themselves Caretakers. We have an internal project name that references the same, a remit about containment and a control module that recognises you. Clearly it has to be connected.”

“Recognises me?” She tasted the word. “Or recognises the thing speaking through me?”

Cuthrick’s tone was gentle. “Either answer argues for extreme caution — and for keeping a proper chain of custody.”

Hale set the wafer beside the unit.&nbsp;

“GalCop was less constrained by rules and regulations. They were the first to really explore the frontier. If they coupled emergent intelligence to hyperspace, those AIs might have somehow survived. GalCop’s collapse was rapid and unstructured. Much knowledge was lost and there were rumours of AIs escaping into the darkness.”

Azalea’s brow furrowed. “You think the Caretakers are… GalCop?”

Jasmina shook her head. “No… that’s not it. But something they did — something survived them.”

Silence took the table for a beat, filled only by distant air handlers and the hush of the Graveyard beyond the glass.

Halsey looked down at the Quirium Control Unit. Ugly and utilitarian, a tool for doing dangerous things in a controlled way — a fitting reliquary for a warning from the dark.&nbsp;

“So what do I do?”

“Take it on your expedition,” Hale said, surprising himself with his certainty. “It belongs with the person it answers. We’ll document the chain of custody. The Alliance will howl, the Empire will raise an eyebrow, the Federation will insist on a copy of the paper trail. Let them. I’ll keep the ledger and wafer. You take the beating heart.”

Azalea’s mouth opened to object; Halsey touched her arm and she closed it again.

“On condition,” Hale added, curator’s caution returning. “You permit instrumented access. We’ll assign a team — mixed, neutral — to observe whenever you interact with it. No one probes the unit without your consent. No one attempts to power it. If it insists on being inert, we respect that.”

“And if it insists on speaking?” Halsey asked.

“Then we listen,” Hale said. “Properly — with numbers as well as ears.”

Cuthrick cleared his throat, diplomatic velvet over iron. “For clarity: Imperial indemnity requires that I witness the transfer and that a duplicate custody log be lodged with the Embassy. No claim of ownership, Doctor — merely record-keeping in line with protocol.”

Hale’s smile was brief and genuine. “Imperial auditors never change. Governance and regulatory supervision… more paperwork.”

“It saves arguments later,” Cuthrick said. “And I fear we’re about to have a great many.”

Hale produced a case lined with non-reactive foam, set the Quirium Control Unit within and sealed it. The faintest impression of pressure brushed Halsey’s palms as the lid clicked shut.

“Jasmina,” Hale said, offering the case. “The Missing left traces in witch-space. If your Caretakers are real — whatever ‘real’ means at this point — this unit may guide you to them. GalCop tried to be careful. They also overreached. History is still being written here. Perhaps now is the time to find those witch-space ghosts and put them to rest.”
