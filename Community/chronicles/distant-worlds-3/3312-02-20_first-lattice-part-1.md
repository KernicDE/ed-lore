Meene, 3312 AD

They were back at their computers, displays and charts. They even had to repurpose an adjacent storage room for more displays and memory banks. All the leads, analyses and models ate more computing power than a Cartographics terminal after one of the Distant Worlds expeditions.

Engineer Ram Tah scratched his head, folding his big brows into the ‘V’ his assistants knew as a sign of deep concern. To his side, his senior assistant Uwe Toramund made some final entries in his personal holofac.

“I need the data from Jaques laid over the data we received yesterday from that carrier. The goo stuff. Then put their flight itinerary to the right and highlight where the expedition did their surveys. Only pick the bigger ones.”

Turning to Toramund, he continued: “Set up telepresence to both send and receive, Tor. Audio only, otherwise it’ll burn through our capacitors.” 

Half an hour later all the displays were set neatly in a mostly helpful arrangement and the base’s TelePro buzzed happily until the receiver sprang to interstellar life. Expectations were high as this was the first telepresence comms attempt after the flotilla’s FTL arrays had been adjusted.

“DW3 ExCom, do you copy?” Ram asked into the void. “This is Meene Base on Echo Comms. Please acknowledge.”

There was an awful lot of noise. Not really static, it was more like a geiger counter complaining about a radiation shower. Then suddenly a beep.

“Yes, we copy. Halsey here and with me are Miss Delarue from DSS and Elaine Howard. We have no request for a visual from you, why do you want audio only?”

“Because the data you sent eats our memory banks and bandwidth. I could buy more memory from the Feds or the Empire but I think it’s a bad idea right now. So we’re somewhat limited. We also can’t get Lya into this, she is still occupied with setting up shop in Orion.”

“It’s working?”

“Well, last I heard she had a heart attack.”

“What?! Because of a few light years?”

“No, one of her instruments lost its calibration or something.”

The head shaking was audible from this side of the galaxy.

“Still,” Ram continued. “Did you manage to get a big picture over there already? We have some ideas we can share but Tor and I also rely on what you think this is all about. After all, you are the visionary here, Madam Envoy. Literally.”

“Vaguely,” Halsey conceded. “And call me Jasmina please. I’m not hosting an Embassy reception. Jaques set us on the trail. He said he witnessed a number of equipment failures inside the station itself and near its perimeter. We had a look around and confirmed it was real, although we couldn’t figure out the reasons. But you already know this.”

“It’s a wonder that station still survives out there, but Jaques’ not only good at serving drinks. When they started to get serious about that patch of space he helped install the very first generation of hardware over there. I reckon no one else would have sensed what was going on there sooner than him.”

“He still clicks his mouth when he gets agitated. He also said the events started to happen when a certain UniCart cruiser came through and uploaded some explo data.”

“The Guinevere? The ship you asked us here to verify?”

“Yes. Did you come up with something?”

“Not much,” a voice echoed from behind the Engineer and his assistant. An old man stepped forward, neat grey suit and blue tinted glasses.

“Interesting. Do I hear a Mister Ermitage?” 

He didn’t bother to answer that.

“I don’t put much trust in the Pilots’ Federation and their cartographers. You know that. Too much bias and too many stock options involved. Still, there are a few things of note. They don’t have many Eridanus-class cruisers because they are specifically designed and equipped for extended deep space survey missions. 

But neither the PF nor UniCart use them very often. They require secondary crews in cryo stasis for such long operations. Too unpopular, too costly and with armies of freelance explorers exposing themselves to the dangers of deep space willingly, such assignments just aren’t necessary anymore. Buying their data is cheaper.”

“But they still had one out there,” came Jasmina’s voice. “And they must have found something. Seems they came across some Guardian beacons while at it and had nothing better to do than get some juicy fighter blueprints?”

“That’s our guess, yes. At least according to what Jaques said. But without flight or cargo manifests we’re not getting anywhere. UniCart won’t tell us.”

“DSSA must have caught the flu then when they met near Rendezvous Point.”

The old man cleared his throat. “Halsey, I realise Miss Delarue has approached my dearest contact on that matter.”

There was a brief pause. “Yes?”

“He won’t tell you anything about it. Don’t waste your breath. His specialty is making obfuscated remarks. He is usually correct in what he says but attunes his wording to what’s expected of him.”

“He knows a lot about GalCop technology.”

“He does. He used to work for them. Well, in a way at least.”

Silence. 

“Are you still there?” 

“Who is this guy?”

The old man’s face darkened. “Believe it or not, he’s still on our side at this point.”

“And I’d never thought I’d say that,” he pensively added. 

“At this point? What on Earth have you set us on here?” came the answer. It was Hermine Delarue’s voice, angry across a thousand light years. “Do you have an idea how dark it’s out here? And I mean in terms of puzzling this all together. Seriously, what were you thinking?”

“Jasmina, you are absolutely on to something,” Ram Tah interrupted. “You need to get to the bottom of this.”

“But why? Why do I even carry this Quirium unit? Is it the Holy Grail to a mythical Avalon? You said you have ideas, models and stuff. Pray tell me, what is going on with your fancy numbers, Ram?”

“Very well,” he said. “First, it’s vital you try and see everything connected, despite distances that seem vast and times that are forgotten.”

“Too lyrical, Ram. What do you mean?”

The Engineer eyed Ermitage and Toramund, then continued: “The gist is, nobody has a clue how Hyperspace — hyperspatial cosmology — really works or if that term really describes what’s going on. Despite its discovery a thousand years ago, we can build engines on a hunch that enable transdimensional portal inception but actual research into the matter hit a snag long ago.”

“Until recently,” Toramund added. “Actually, the data you provided us with made it possible to refine a number of theories that had been shelved. Too outlandish at that time and… too non-conformist for major league industrial donors.”

“You mean frameshift tech?”

“Not precisely. Early Einsteinian relativity assumes space and time were inseparably entwined and on the same spacetime lattice. It’s a crude theory but it has worked for a long time. But some think they are separable and that hyperspace, or more precisely hypertemporal space, is actually the not so theoretical result of this split.”

“I don’t understand that at all.”

“It’s pretty new to us,” the Engineer said. “Ask Lya. She is one of the few who already have a degree in Temporal Theory. The ‘Temps’ say hypertemporal space exists everywhere at once. In their equations, time has a value of zero, meaning a hypothetical point B in space can be reached from point A instantaneously. Or events that are entangled, for lack of a better term. That’s why we think all the spectacle you encountered is connected, but in a way we don’t yet understand.”

“You say it’s new to us. You think someone has actually developed this hypertemporal tech in secret?” Jasmina asked, more or less rhetorically.

“No. Not remotely possible. At least not by human standards and that’s where it gets interesting and why I said us.”

“You mean I’m not going to say it’s aliens but it’s the aliens?”

“Sarcastic but pretty accurate. We correlated all the data from the Guardian sites last November with the events that occurred near that timestamp. Interestingly, you collapsed in Congress under duress of your first returning vision when our dear explorers started scrutinizing the obelisks. And there were a few thousand light years in between so our theories and hopes it’s a local phenomenon are void — literally.”

“I know what you’re aiming at but that doesn’t explain why that Tionisla marvel resonates whenever something happens or is about to happen.”

“I think it does. The lattice on which these events happen over vast distances, and more or less simultaneously, is remarkably similar to what we think we know about Guardian communication, which was heavily reliant on the use of their version of artificial intelligence — if they actually regarded it as such.”

“And?”

“And your Reuben Hale in Tionisla alluded that your Quirium Control Unit held an at least semi-autonomous assistance firmware at some point, meant to regulate the complex process of Quirium injection, while preventing the process from becoming uppity.”   

“And you think the unit responds to what you call a hyper… hyper… Hell, I can’t even remember what it was! That lattice, because someone wants to control it? I remember Hale saying some GalCop projects ‘forgot themselves’. I thought that was a joke in Delaney’s direction for being…”

“Uppity?”

“Yeah.”

“For what it’s worth, at this time I don’t think your Quirium Control Unit responds to something. I think it’s the other way round. Something tries to make contact with both you and the unit. And this something, in not so scientifical terms, is literally all over the place and at the same time. It resides in hypertemporal space — witch-space. All indications lead to this conclusion.”

“The visions…”

“Exactly. You said on Mars you felt a fabric under tension and voices warning you about something in witch-space. You called them Caretakers, which implies a sentience with a moral compass and an understanding of community, and that they were afraid. We know of one species out there whose society reflected these concepts. Apparently, those weren’t metaphors, Jasmina.”

“The Guardians.”

“Yes.”

“And the degenerate compounds we found on the DSSA carrier and later the evacuated T-9? They were here, not in witch-space. Another thing forgetting it existed until summoned from the depths of the Archer’s Shadow”?

At this point you could hear rolling eyes on the bridge of one of the distant carriers.

“We don’t know yet. We simply don’t. We sadly have no physical sample so have to rely on remote methods. Preliminary analysis suggests the materials were broken down on a molecular level, including organic matter. It’s probably not radiation but rather age related decomposition. Decay; natural decay but in materials that either don’t have one or one that’s exceedingly slow. But that doesn’t explain the structures you refer to as ‘nano goo’ and it doesn’t explain why the T-9 wasn’t wholly consumed.”

“And the crew?”

“Whatever is at work here, it’s probably easier to break down organic matter than plastisteel or proto-alloys. You really found some nightmare stuff there, Jasmina.” 

“We need to make sure no one finds it, not in a lifetime. Not ever. We are going to have DSSA destroy it.”

“Probably the best course of action,” the Engineer said.

“Still, if what you say is true,” Jasmina added, “the technology is out there somewhere waiting to be found. I know when GalCop collapsed a lot of their research was sold off to the highest bidder by the remaining opportunists. Yes, some accuse the Alliance and especially the Turners but it’s foolish to assume there weren’t others. Ermitage, I think you would agree with my completely unfounded statement?”

Another proof sarcasm could be transmitted over vast distances without loss of aim and context.

“It’s very, very unfounded, Halsey. People love you for your casual statements that have filled the dirt files of your political adversaries.”

“It’s the skeletons we make along the way.”

“Jasmina, you do politics,” the Engineer interrupted again. “You say tech but this thing probably isn’t even working technology. I doubt that material decay is on purpose. If you want to invent a word, call it meta-tech. But I don’t need to tell you whoever gets a hold on this would be swimming in trillions upon trillions of credits by next Friday.”

“I doubt the Guardians or my Quirium Unit are interested in trillions of credits.”

“Probably not but I have an idea. I need to find out something, although it may be increasingly difficult to make unseen moves.”

“What do you mean?”

“Well, you agreed to share your findings with all the committees in the world, remember?”

“Yes?”

Ram Tah looked at Ermitage, eyebrows raised. Stepping forward again, he answered the obvious concern: “The last dossiers we filed were answered with some very detailed questionnaires, kindly asking for more information. Ambassador Delaney and I try to stall them, bury them with nothingburgers. But it won’t last forever. When political politeness fails, you suddenly become a person of interest and narratives begin to shift.”

The Engineer chimed in again. “We’re pretty safe here, rest assured. I still have people who owe me. However, before scrutiny increases I need to corroborate something. Something you probably shouldn’t concern yourself with. It’s going to be here in the Bubble anyway so just continue your jaunt and keep us informed.”

“You’re not going to do anything stupid, Ram, are you?”

“Me? No. I have an army of ghosts for that.”
