Abyss Region, 3312 AD

The galaxy did not end with a wall.

It thinned.

That was the only honest word for it. The density of stars that had once crowded every canopy began to loosen, their patient abundance giving way to widening gaps. Navigation plots grew sparse. Fuel calculations grew cautious. Plotting computers buzzed with impotence. The great spiral that had held humanity’s attention for centuries no longer surrounded the Distant Worlds flotilla. It lay behind them now, like the memory of a crowded room after the door had closed.

Beagle point had been reached. It was pretty much the edge, looking past that was looking into pitch darkness with no end.

Behind lay everything else.

The convoy stretched across supercruise in a slow, deliberate procession, thousands of ships moving with the careful restraint of travellers who understood, perhaps too late, how far from home they had come. Carrier wakes braided faintly in the void, their geometry precise and fragile, while smaller vessels darted between them like fish sheltering beneath larger shadows.

From the cockpit of her Caspian explorer, the USS Ceilidh, Alessia Verdi watched the procession with the distant detachment of someone who had long ago learned to distrust the romance of exploration.

She had joined the expedition late, catching one of the last fleet carrier departures from the Bubble as it burned its way outward toward Colonia and beyond. She had told no one why. There had been no point. Pilots rarely asked questions whose answers they did not want.

Now she leaned against the canopy rail, arms folded, eyes half-lidded, watching the convoy with the clinical patience of a veteran soldier observing new recruits discover their limits.

She knew they were beginning to feel it. All of them.

Not fear, exactly.

Something quieter.

Something older.

Fatigue did not arrive suddenly in deep space. It accumulated, imperceptibly at first. Sleep cycles drifted. Concentration wavered. Small mistakes appeared, corrected quickly and quietly, but not forgotten. Conversations repeated themselves. Pilots found themselves staring at instrument panels without remembering what they had meant to check.

In the carrier concourses and mess halls, speculation had replaced celebration.

The discovery of the nameless carrier drifting near the Praei permit boundary had left a bruise on the expedition’s collective psyche, and the still unknown mission of the Guinevere deepened it into something harder to ignore. The official bulletins remained calm, precise and reassuring, but the gaps between those words had begun to speak more loudly than the words themselves.

Alessia had heard the questions. They were good questions.

Why K-type stars? Why neutron stars now? Why had a prototype carrier been built around a hybrid Quirium architecture that no one officially knew how to manufacture? Why had it been empty? Why had it been alive? Secret comms back to engineers in the bubble, about what?

The questions circulated in bars and corridors and private comm channels, accumulating like static.

She didn’t volunteer her own speculation. It was too early for that. Experience had taught her that answers arrived when they chose to, not when they were demanded.

But she knew the symptoms well enough. Deep space missions always went like this.

The space madness was starting to show.

---
Jasmina felt it too.

Not in her mind.

In her bones.

She stood alone in the quiet of her quarters, the sealed Quirium Control Unit resting on the table before her like an object that had not yet decided whether it was a tool or a warning.

It had been silent since the discovery of the carrier. That silence troubled her more than its voice ever had.

She had come to recognise its cadence, its strange, hesitant attempts at communication, the subtle misalignment in reality that accompanied its moments of awareness. Now it lay inert, its gold mesh and ceramic planes absorbing the soft cabin lighting without response.

She wondered if it was listening. Or waiting. Or hiding.

Beyond the bulkheads, the convoy moved steadily towards the next waypoint, each jump bringing them closer to the edge of mapped space and further into regions where charts ended and extrapolation began.

She had reviewed the medical reports earlier that cycle. The medical staff were playing it down, but she knew they were worried. Deep Space Dissociation Syndrome.

The name sounded clinical, manageable, safe. The reality was none of those things.

Pilots reported hearing voices buried in sensor noise. Engineers described moments of disorientation while performing routine diagnostics. Service crew reported dreams that persisted into waking, fragments of conversations and places that vanished when examined directly.

Individually, each report could be dismissed. Together, they formed a pattern.

Human cognition had evolved beneath skies that provided context. Horizons. Reference points. Boundaries. Out here, those boundaries no longer existed.

The mind adapted poorly to infinity. Generation ships had discovered that long ago. Their silent hulls still drifted in forgotten systems, monuments to optimism and endurance and the quiet, inevitable erosion of certainty.

Jasmina wondered if those early crews had felt it too. The sense of being observed by something that wasn’t using eyes.

---
In Odysseus' primary bar, the mood had shifted from celebration to endurance.

Pilots gathered out of habit rather than enthusiasm, drawn by gravity that had nothing to do with mass.

Jacky Hew held court near the observation viewport, his voice carrying the rough confidence of someone who had spent too long trusting his instincts to stop now.

“Carrier jumps,” he was saying, gesturing vaguely toward the stars, “they’re different. Smoother. Cleaner. Back in my day, when I scouted the neutron highway, you felt every transition. Your ship complained. Your bones complained. This new tech… it’s like something else is doing the work for you.”

Someone laughed nervously.

“You saying you miss the old days?”

Jacky grinned, but the expression didn’t quite reach his eyes.

“I’m saying,” he replied, “that when things get easier, you stop asking why. When you see the void start staring back at you, that’s the time to pop the pills.”

Alessia, having docked aboard, listened from her usual corner, nursing a drink she had no intention of finishing.

She recognised the tone around her. It was familiar enough from her adventures in the Formidine Rift. She shivered.

Not fear. Not yet. But… unease.

The first cracks were appearing.

---
Jasmina returned to the bridge and stood at the observation rail and watched the slow procession of ships preparing for the next jump cycle. Thousands of independent pilots, scientists, soldiers and dreamers, all suspended at the edge of known space.

They had come seeking discovery. They had found questions instead.

Behind her, unnoticed, the Quirium Control Unit trembled once.

A single missed beat.

Then silence.

Jasmina did not turn.

She was already listening.

And somewhere beyond the reach of charts and permits and human certainty, something else was listening too.
