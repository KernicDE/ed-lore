Colonia, 3312 AD

The big ships slid in on tidy vectors, heat blooms restrained, wakes braided so as not to rattle the locals. After the first week the corridor had stopped bucking. The fifteen-cycle miss still showed up now and then, a smudge in the low band, but it had become background hiss. Pilots stopped murmuring about ghosts and went back to arguing about jumponium routes, the latest SRV racing results and who had the better synth-coffee machines on their Kraits.

By the time the convoy crossed the last stretch to the Colonia Bridge, bar talk had cooled. A notorious carouser named Jacky Hew decided to fix that. Jacky held court on all of the four Distant Worlds carriers, making the rounds and gathering in the drinks.

“Carrier jumps are different, kids,” he announced to the room, pint aloft. “You pack your whole bad decision into a moving rock and fling it at the sky. No romance. Now, back in the day when I scouted the Neutron Highway—”

Groans rose like a litany from the assembled crowds, but they were up for a bit of entertainment after being cooped up in their cockpits for days on end. Jasmina sat at the back, inconspicuous, and happy to be part of the scenery. But she watched everything going on around her. People watching, particularly spacers and pilots, could reveal things…

“—you learned the smell of your FSD cooking itself to jerky. You snorted cone plots like spice. You rode the superflares till your eyebrows went wobbly. If the ship didn’t try to kill you twice before breakfast you were still docked.”

Someone shouted that Jacky had been docked more often than not. Jacky bowed to the accusation and drank to it.

“Point is,” he said, leaning in as if sharing state secrets, “I’ve seen drives sing when they shouldn’t, and I’ve seen them sulk when paid. But I’ve also heard weird whispers out here. Kit going wonky across a few Colonia stations. Docking computers that fancy themselves poets. Cartographic terminals freezing like they remembered something they shouldn’t.”

That last line found more ears than it should have. Jasmina leant forward a touch.

“Maybe someone ought to have a look around,” Jacky added, casual and sharp at once. “That’s all I’m saying…”

Jaques Station filled the canopy like a huge misshapen scar of a station, skin stippled with repair plates, lights running along its flanks like the memory of tides. Halsey took the call from a quiet corner of the Caspian ship’s bridge rather than her cabin. It felt right to keep the stars in view while speaking to a man who had once flown himself across half the galaxy.

The holo resolved to a face part flesh part alloy. Servo-lines traced from a chrome cheek-plate to the temple where a pale optical implant irised against the link’s light. A sub-vocal modulator gave his words a second undertone like valves warming.

“Madam Envoy,” Jaques said, amused rather than formal. The metal in his jaw clicked once as he smiled. “Welcome back to the colony that isn’t a colony.”

“Just a waypoint that learned to have opinions,” Halsey said. “Good to see you, Jaques.”

“Likewise. I hear you brought an entire entourage with you. Try not to jam all the pads?’

“We will be polite,” she promised. “I need your help. We have been chasing a cadence. I hear there’s been the odd strange thing noted at your end of the map.”

Jaques cocked his head. Tiny servos whirred as the optical implant refocused. “You heard about the gear behaving like it grew feelings?”

“Jacky Hew was flapping his mouth,” she said.

“Yes, he does that,” Jaques replied, the modulator colouring the consonants. “He is not wrong about this though. We have had intermittent misbehaviour. Nothing that breaks a ship. Everything that irritates an engineer. Docking towers that refuse a pad allocation until you swear at them. Power relays that forget their friends. The odd nav-buoy claiming it is a cargo rack. My favourite is a voice alarm that went from traffic advisory to singing a line from some ancient Scottish melody. No obvious pattern, but there is a shape to the places.”

“Which places?”

“Started as whispers at outposts along the lane,” Jaques said. He glanced off-screen and a retinal HUD flickered in the implant’s lens. “Then we started hearing it in the station. And the timing, Jasmina. Odd. No spikes during your approach. But when the Pilots’ Federation exploration cruiser came through. Big. Proud. Eridanus class. Bristling with long-range survey kit. They did the round of the local systems. Next morning they uploaded a mountain of data to Universal Cartographics. Top tier. Straight to my station. Cost me a fortune. After that the gremlins woke up.”

“You’re sure about the timing?”

“All bartenders have good clocks,” he said. The metal jaw clicked again, a habit more than a fault. “They walked the data to UC in the afternoon. That night I started getting reports of nonsense. Day after that two ships reported their surface scanners were hearing background music on surface scans. Just weird behaviour…”

“Any common fit in the pattern of weirdness?”

“Mostly modern drives,” Jaques said. “Half the ships here have bolted Guardian wake tat to their hulls in the last few years so we see a lot of hybrids. I cannot prove correlation. I can tell you two PF officers were in the bar talking about a Guardian Beacon out in the dark. Said it was ‘strange’ when they pinged it on their way in. One of them mentioned a DSSA carrier further north on the map. They want to liaise before they go and spend two years making friends with K-type stars.”

“Strange...” Jasmina said. “Like you say, lots of pieces, no pattern. Can we help? What do you need?”

“What I need is for everything to behave,” Jaques said with a dry little harmonics-wobble from the modulator. “Failing that, get your massive bunch of scientists to scan my house and garden. Keep it honest. Run a wake net round the station. Sniff my nav-buoys. Sample the EM in the docking approach.”

“Perimeter or close?”

“Both. Hell, scan everything in Colonia if you have the time. We need to know if this is local mischief or if the smear has a radius,” he said. The eye-implant shuttered and opened. “I can give you priority station time.”

“I’ll see what we can do.”

“And if your numbers match the gossip you will want to know what those PF officers saw when they went poking at Guardian hardware.”

“I will want to know that anyway. Do you have their names?” Jasmina asked.

“The cruiser left but I still have their call signs on a cocktail bill. I will forward them. And a suggestion.” A faint servo-hum underscored the pause. “If you hear echoes from the the void do not stand still too long. Whatever is making our wires itch did not start here.”

“Agreed.”

“Good. Dock your expedition,” Jaques said. The alloy cheek caught the holo’s glow as he dipped his head. “And Jasmina… if the ghosts want to sing, please make them keep to key. By the way, you should visit my bar. There’s someone here I think you should meet.”

Jasmina was about to ask who, but the holofac communication had already flickered off.

The bar sat in the crook of a support rib where the station’s bones showed through the cladding. Lamps made from old nav beacons threw warm cones of light. The room smelled of coolant, burnt coffee and citrus cleaner that tried its best. A low thread of conversation ran under a shipboard blues track that never quite resolved.

Jaques eased through the crowd with the easy authority of a man who had poured a million drinks. Jasmina followed him through the jostling mass of people. The alloy in Jaques’ jaw clicked once as he smiled.

“Jasmina,” he said, pitching his voice for her ear alone. “Over here. Her name is Alessia Verdi. Imperial. Flies like she has somewhere to be and a grudge for company. She’s been here a few times before. Knows her ships. Has stories… if she’s here in Colonia, it means something. She only just made the last fleet carrier out of the bubble – last minute decision to tag along? Might be related, might not. Worth checking.”

Jasmina looked where Jaques gestured.

The woman at the bar rail had the lean look of long patrols and stress. Flight leathers scuffed white at the seams. A silver signet at her throat. She watched the room as if the room might try something. When she turned her head Halsey saw the small Imperial crest tattooed just inside the hairline. Old ink. Earned, not bought. Grey hair and grey eyes marked a face that had been young not that long ago, but had been aged prematurely by grief and strife.

Jaques introduced them, providing both with a drink.

“Madam Envoy,” Alessia said. The vowels were clipped Achenar. “Or do you prefer Jasmina like the GalNet liars?”

“Jasmina will do,” Halsey said. “Jaques tells me you barely made the last ride out of the bubble.”

A shrug. “I’m fashionably late. I do not like crowds. The fleet is a crowd with engines. Colonia was quieter before. This expedition changed that.”

“You came anyway.”

“I have my reasons.” Alessia said. “It’s clearer out here in the void. Jaques’ whisky is bad, but the coffee is ok.”

Jaques let that sit. Alessia gave him a suggestive look. The servos behind his eye implant ticked as he checked a ghost HUD only he could see. “I will refresh my reputation with the bar,” he said mildly, and drifted away.

Alessia watched him go then put her elbows on the rail. “He is smarter than he looks,” she said. “And a lot older than most realise.”

Halsey took the spot beside her. The window behind the bar showed a sliver of the docking axis. A Type-9 drifted through the slot like a stubborn planet.

“Jaques said you had history,” Halsey said. “That you’re someone I should know.”

“History is the polite word,” Alessia said. Her mouth did not soften. “You have heard the latest rumours. The dead walk. Titles crawl out of graves. Salomé returns to judge the living.”

“Kahina Tijani Loren,” Halsey said. “The enigmatic Salomé. I remember the broadcasts. But that was years ago. She was shot down… some say she committed suicide, others that she faked her death and is still alive…”

Alessia’s knuckles whitened on the rail. The tendons in her hands drew tight, then let go. “I was there,” she said. “I saw her die. Not a myth. Not a holo. Blood and vacuum. She’s gone.”

Jasmina could see the haunted look in the other woman’s eyes.

“You followed her.”

“She was my friend.”

Jasmina could see Alessia’s jaws clench. She looked away before speaking again.

“I believed she would make an Empire worth fighting for,” Alessia said. “Expose the wrongs… and right them. I was wrong about that – wrong about a lot of things. Salomé failed. She let us down, all of us. Then others made a business out of her death. She was not the paragon of virtue she has been idolised as. I was caught up in the worship like so many others, I was young, stupid and naive. I made mistakes. I’ve paid for them ever since. I don’t follow saviours anymore.”

The bitterness was clean. Not theatrical. A slow acid that had eaten its way to daylight.

“Then why chase a convoy into deep space,” Halsey asked. “Why are you here? Because of Salomé?”

“Because I know what it looks like when a story gets written over the top of the truth,” Alessia said. “Your fleet is going somewhere important. Half the officers in every club from Cubeo to Sothis are already writing the ending for you. I would rather see what is really happening. I don’t know what you are chasing, probably I don’t need to, but this is not just an exploration trip and it feels like it did back then… secrets in the darkness waiting to be found. Danger lurking, people scheming… I had to come.”

They let the music hold the next moment. The singer found a line about lost systems and debts unpaid. Halsey watched Alessia’s face find and lose a dozen replies.

“You know the Empire,” Halsey said. “Imperial currents. Who listens. Who pretends not to. If I needed to put a word in a specific ear without leaving fingerprints could you help me?”

Alessia did not look surprised. “You mean could you use me as a lever?”

“I was trying to be subtle.”

“I have a highly tuned bullshit detector,” Alessia snapped. “I knew the moment Jaques offered me a free drink. Everyone wants something, you’re no different.”

“If it keeps pilots alive,” Halsey said. “If it keeps the science honest...”

A long look. Not hostile. Measuring weight and cost.

“I am not a diplomat,” Alessia said at last. “I am good at making a message feel like gossip and letting it land where it should. I can tell you who is compromised and who isn’t. I can do that. But I will not lie for you – you’re not worth it, no one is.”

“I will not ask you to,” Halsey said.

“Good,” Alessia said. “Then here is some free advice. Those who trade in legends are already tacking Salomé’s name to your story. They will say the Caretakers spoke to her first, or some other spacer crap. They will say she’s somehow wrapped up in all this, that she’s running the expedition in secret – they love their conspiracy theories! They still treat her like a fallen goddess whose name will never die. You will not win by arguing with that. You will only win by using that to your advantage.”

Halsey felt a small involuntary smile. “Hermine will approve of your method.”

“Who?”

“Hermine Delarue, our operations lead,” Halsey said. “She hates anything she can’t stick a gauge on.”

“Then she and I will get along if we never meet,” Alessia said. The corners of her mouth twitched. It did not get as far as a smile.

Jaques returned with two short glasses and a nod to the tender. Steam rose from one. The other held something that looked like it had been aged inside a thruster bell.

“House policy,” he said. “First drink after a long road is on me if you promise not to break my station. You will like the coffee. Alessia will pretend not to like the whisky.”

Alessia took the glass and did not pretend. “Salud,” she said. “No legends, just people.”

“No legends,” Halsey echoed.

They drank. The whisky carried a quiet burn that did not argue. The coffee was bitter in a way that felt useful.

Alessia set her glass down and faced Halsey without the rail between them. “If you find a truth out there,” she said, “let me know and say it plain. We can work with plain, we choke on riddles.”

“I will say it plain,” Halsey said. “And if I need a word whispered?”

“I’m on the expedition,” Alessia replied. “I’ll be at the back most likely. Keeping an eye on things. You’ll be able to find me.”

Jasmina nodded.

“Oh, and one more thing,” Alessia said.

Jasmina looked back at her.

“Do not underestimate the power of myth,” Alessia said. “Salomé is dead, but in all the years since that hasn’t slowed her down one bit…”

Halsey kept her own role small. She visited the tower to drink station air and thank the crews for accommodating the mass of DW3 ships flooding the docks. She walked the concourse and watched the flow around the terminals without stepping too close. She stood on a viewing deck where the window glass had been etched by a thousand small collisions and looked down at the moving geometry of approach lights and landing gear.

When the first returns came in they were boring, which is what you want your first returns to be. A clean wake net. EM sweeps with a few hairs in the comb where the spines hummed against their brackets. Buoy traffic within noise tolerances, save for a packet with a timing smear that a junior tech flagged as “ugly but harmless.”

Then a pilot called Alistair Brodey, flying an Eagle with an engineered wake scanner, pinged a comm.

“Operations, you want this,” Brodey said, half-whispered awe threading through professionalism. “Wake scanner just picked up… something. Cross-band echo at fifteen cycles. Same miss. It is faint but it is there.”

Hermine’s reply was a heartbeat quick, silk turned to wire. “Brodey? Didn’t know you were out there. Tag, stamp, push raw. Repeat sweep. Anyone else with a wake focus near that local, get me more data.”

Two more ships caught it on the next pass. Not loud. Not dramatic. A fingertip on a drum. Fifteen.

Halsey stood very still in the crowd and imagined a lattice pulled like a net over a town, small ripples turning cutlery in drawers, lights that forget their names for a second then remember. She imagined a cruiser bristling with instruments walking a mountain of maps into a hungry machine and the hungry machine humming back in a key no one could hear until now.

Her console chimed. A text from Jaques. Thought so… whatever this is, it’s not from here, but it’s been here…

Halsey exhaled, steady. “Captain Yuen,” she said into the ship line, “let’s see where the nice PF cruiser went sightseeing. Jaques said ‘north’ and DSSA is involved so give them a call. And send the wake scans home to Lya, see what she can figure out. She still has all the Guardian data from that Engineer. See if it sings out of tune.”

The tug at her ribs had not strengthened, but it had learned to tap once in every so many beats. Colonia was a pause and a welcome rest, not an answer.

“On your mark,” the captain said.

“Engage,” Halsey replied, and the DW3 machine prepared to face the next question.
