Tionisla, after Launch Day

The scenery outside was melancholic; the system’s orange sun was far away and it barely illuminated all the bizarre metal carcasses. The place was full of them, as far as the eye — or the ship canopy’s enhanced visual sensors — could see; hundreds if not thousands of ancient space hulks and star fighters, floating at one of the Lagrange points in the star system’s complicated orbits. Derelict ships of all models and ages slowly rotated in a perennial danse macabre, disturbed only occasionally by the local security patrols that struggled to deter scavengers, adventurous tourists and, more recently, Federation officials and their patchwork entourage. 

One of the ships deep inside this belt of metal corpses danced the same dance, but it was intact and occupied. It rolled with a gentle five percent pitch, perfectly mimicking its defunct companions. Every once in a while the shadows of its bigger neighbours fell over the hull, covering it in utter darkness. A few minutes later they would retreat again to let the dim sunlight reflect off the ship’s hull once more. 

Inside, most of its systems had been deactivated, including the cockpit lights. Observers would likely have smiled at the pair of wax candles on a candelabra resting on the dashboard. The flickering lights danced across the cockpit’s consoles and, along with the ship’s gentle roll, they seemed to hold its pilot in a trance. He sat in the pilot’s chair, apparently oblivious to his surroundings.

Soft music filled the cockpit with the delicate and ancient sound of string instruments: Le Quattro Stagioni, the four seasons, an impossibly old piece of art composed by a long-dead genius, who would never have imagined his concerti would be listened to inside a lone spaceship more than 1,500 years later.  

The pilot was young for a Commander, although his outfit suggested otherwise: he wore some sort of outdated dark grey and green uniform with fingerless gloves. His flight suit may have been fashionable in the eighties. He was not tall, and had short, curly, black hair. A moustache and a chin beard completed the sly, roguish appearance. 

An observer may have guessed he was meditating to the intense music. He absently held a glass of red wine in his right hand, resting his chin in his left. The glass had a lid and a straw. Every once in a while he turned the glass a few degrees and looked at the wine drops bouncing inside. The music and tranquil setting seemed to consume him wholly.

Suddenly, one of the console lights flashed red, and an audio-mechanic beep disrupted the melodious ambience. 

“Ah, no! No, no, no!”

Annoyed, the pilot exploded from his meditation, nearly letting the glass go. With a hissed curse he shook his head, reached forward and switched one of the small mechanical levers on the console.

Abruptly, the music ceased. He sat upright and pulled his flight seat closer to the console. A ‘click’ somewhere signalled the re-routing of the ship’s comm system and next, a single, distorted voice echoed from the speakers:

“Atra rota. Mundi distantes in motu sunt. Procedite.”

Still annoyed, the pilot put down his glass of red wine, reached over to the console again and switched the lever in the opposite direction.

“Okay, okay. Digli che sto arrivando.”

The pilot put his glass in one of the cup-holders to his right and blew out the candles. He pulled one of the overhead levers and the ship stopped to roll. When he was sure the rotation had come to a halt he cautiously moved the power controller forward until the navigation panel came online. A number of blips appeared in a holographic sphere above the panel. He took a few moments to study their movements; they were security patrols on duty, their trajectories routine. 

“Merda.”

He activated the bow thrusters. There was the sound of machinery in the bowels of his venerable ship, as it began to move backwards. When it was clear of a neighbouring Boa freighter, the pilot relaxed. The huge ship would provide him with a decent sensor shadow to all but the keenest observers. Finally, he powered the hyperdrive and began his calculations for a jump trajectory. 

A few minutes later he was gone and the ship cemetery was left to itself again.
